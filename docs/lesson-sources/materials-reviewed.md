## Materials 101 · Lesson 1: The four bonds

Physics told you how loads move through parts. Materials explains **why steel bends, ceramic shatters, and plastic goes soft when hot**. The answer starts with how the atoms are stuck together.

### 1. The one question to ask

For any material: **what is free to move?**

- **Electrons?** → it conducts electricity
- **Planes of atoms or whole chains?** → it bends or stretches instead of cracking
- **No easy charge transport or slip?** → expect low electrical conductivity and brittle behaviour; check exceptions such as semiconductors.

### 2. The four bond types

| Bond | How atoms hold on | What can move | So it's… | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Metallic** | atoms share a "sea" of loose electrons | **electrons** and **slip planes** | conductive, **bends** (ductile) | copper, steel, aluminium |
| **Ionic** | one atom gives electrons to another, making + and − ions that attract | **nothing** (in the solid) | insulator, **brittle**, high melting point | table salt, magnesium oxide |
| **Covalent network** | atoms share electron pairs in fixed directions, in one giant connected structure | little easy slip | often insulating or semiconducting, **brittle**, high-temperature resistance | diamond, silicon carbide |
| **Secondary** | weak attraction **between** molecules or chains | **whole chains** slide past each other | soft, low melting point, softens with heat | polyethylene, wax |

### 3. Why metals bend

The electron "sea" doesn't care about direction. Rows of atoms can **slide past each other** and the bonding just re-forms. That sliding is **ductility**: why you can bend a steel bar or draw copper into wire.

**One feature, two properties:** the same loose electrons that let metal **bend** also let it **conduct**. That's why conductive materials and bendable materials are usually the same ones.

### 4. Why ionic materials shatter

Ions sit in a checkerboard: + − + −. Try to slide one row over by half a step and suddenly **+ faces +** and **− faces −**. They repel and the crystal **cracks apart** instead of bending.

The attractive **energy** scales with charge × charge ÷ distance; the attractive **force** scales with charge × charge ÷ distance². Neither alone is a bulk-strength formula. Doubly charged ions close together (Mg²⁺, O²⁻) hold **much** harder than singly charged ones (Na⁺, Cl⁻).

**Odd fact:** solid salt doesn't conduct, but **molten** salt does, because then the ions themselves can move.

### 5. Covalent: the structure matters

Covalent bonds are strong and **directional** (they point at specific angles). What matters is how they're connected:

| Structure | To melt it, you must… | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Network** (diamond, SiC) | break the strong bonds themselves | extremely high melting point, brittle |
| **Separate molecules** | just pull the molecules apart (weak forces) | low melting point |

### 6. Secondary bonds: why plastics soften

In polyethylene, the bonds **along** each chain are strong, but the chains only cling to **each other** weakly. Secondary bonds are roughly **10–100× weaker** than primary bonds.

Heat it and the chains start **sliding past each other** before anything breaks. That's why plastics soften and creep, and why a plastic part near an engine can sag.

### 7. What the animation shows

| Step | Panel | What moves |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Metallic** | electrons drifting through + ions | electrons ✔, slip planes ✔ |
| **2. Ionic, covalent** | locked + − lattice; fixed shared pairs | nothing |
| **3. Secondary** | long chains loosely held | whole chains slide |
| **4. What moves** | side-by-side comparison | the answer predicts the properties |

### 8. The worked prediction: magnesium oxide (MgO)

Mg gives **two** electrons to O → Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ (**ionic**, double charges).

- No loose electrons → **insulator**
- Sliding brings like charges together → **brittle**
- Double charges, tightly packed → predict a **high melting temperature**; **about 2,850 °C is a supplied reference value**, not a result calculated from the bond.

That's why it's used to **line furnaces**. The qualitative property package follows from the bonding. The numerical melting temperature must be looked up.

### 9. Where you'll see this in the shop

- **Many ductile steel parts bend** before breaking; hardened or embrittled tools may fracture without that warning
- **Grinding wheels and ceramic inserts** are hard but **chip and shatter** if dropped (covalent or ionic)
- **Plastic parts** soften and deform near heat (secondary bonds between chains)

### 10. The method

1. **Name the bond:** metallic, ionic, covalent (network or molecular), or secondary.
2. **Ask what moves:** electrons? slip planes? chains? nothing?
3. **Predict:** conductive or not, bends or shatters, high or low melting point.
4. If it's a mix, name both.

**Try one:** predict conductivity, bend vs shatter, and melting point for:

- (a) aluminium
- (b) silicon carbide (the abrasive on grinding wheels)
- (c) candle wax

You should get (a) metallic: conducts, bends, moderate melting point; (b) covalent network: brittle and heat-resistant; silicon carbide is a semiconductor, so conductivity needs more than the bonding label; (c) secondary bonds between molecules: insulator, soft, low melting point.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 2: Why properties travel together

The last lesson named the four bonds. This one shows that **a material's properties aren't separate facts**. Melting point, stiffness, conductivity and ductility all come from the same bond, so they tend to come **as a package**.

### 1. The bond-energy curve: a valley

Picture two atoms. Push them too close and they repel. Pull them apart and they attract. In between is a sweet spot where they're happiest. On a graph of energy vs distance, that's a **valley** (the page calls it a "well").

Think of a marble sitting at the bottom of a valley:

| Feature of the valley | What it controls |
|---|---|
| **Depth** (how far to climb out) | **melting point**: how much heat it takes to shake the atoms apart |
| **Steepness at the bottom** (how quickly it curves up) | **stiffness (E)**: how hard the bond pushes back when you stretch it |

**One curve, two properties.** A deep, narrow valley means high melting **and** stiff. A shallow, wide one means low melting **and** floppy.

### 2. What the animation shows

| Step | Valley | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Deep well** | tungsten, ~850 kJ/mol | melts at **3,422 °C**, very stiff |
| **2. Shallow well** | secondary bonds, 1–40 kJ/mol | soft, low melting (waxes, many plastics) |
| **3. Compare** | | same shape of curve, wildly different depth |

### 3. Melting point follows bond strength (roughly)

| Material | Bond energy (roughly) | Melting |
|---|---|---|
| Wax | ~1 kJ/mol | ~60 °C |
| Tungsten | ~850 | 3,422 °C |
| MgO | ~3,800 | ~2,850 °C |

**This is a trend, not a formula.** Use it to say "hot," "warm" or "barely above room temperature," not to calculate an exact melting point. (MgO has a bigger number than tungsten but melts lower. The scatter is real.)

### 4. Stiffness is bond stiffness, scaled up

Each bond acts like a tiny **spring**. A material's modulus E is basically those atomic springs added up:

**E ≈ bond stiffness ÷ bond length**

- **Diamond:** deep, narrow valley → extremely stiff
- **Polymers:** shallow secondary bonds between chains → flexible

This explains the E values from the physics block: steel 200 GPa, aluminium 69, wood ~10. Aluminium melts at 660 °C and iron at 1,538 °C. The one with the deeper valley is also the stiffer one.

### 5. Tungsten: one bond, two opposite effects

Tungsten's very strong metallic bonds mean:

- ✔ **Survives huge heat** (highest melting point of any metal), which is why it's used for lamp filaments and TIG welding electrodes
- ✘ **Hard to bend at room temperature**, because strong, short bonds make slip expensive, so it's brittle-ish cold

The same strength that helps with heat **hurts** ductility. Properties travel together, and sometimes that's a trade-off.

### 6. Mixed bonding means a mixed package

Some materials have **two** kinds of bond, and their properties **split by direction**:

**Graphite** (pencil lead):

| Direction | Bond | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Within each sheet** | covalent (strong) | stiff, conducts electricity |
| **Between sheets** | secondary (weak) | sheets slide off easily; conducts much less well across the layers |

That's why a pencil writes: sheets peel off onto the paper. It's also why graphite works as a dry lubricant.

**Glass** is part ionic, part covalent, so it's brittle and insulating, with a fairly high softening point.

**When properties seem contradictory, look for a second bond.**

### 7. The chain for the whole course

**Processing changes structure → structure sets properties → properties affect performance**

Bonding is the first link in "structure." The rest of the course adds the other links: crystal arrangement, heat treatment, and so on.

### 8. The method

1. **Name the bond** (or bonds).
2. **List what it allows:** electrons moving? planes slipping? chains sliding?
3. **Predict the package:**
   - deep valley → high melting, stiff
   - loose electrons → conductive **and** ductile
   - shallow bonds between chains → soft, low melting
4. **If one property doesn't fit**, look for a second bond and work out which property belongs to which bond.

**Try one:**

- (a) Graphite and diamond are both pure carbon. Why does graphite write on paper but diamond scratches glass?
- (b) A plastic part sags on a hot dashboard. Which bond is giving up?

You should get (a) graphite has weak secondary bonds between its sheets, which slide off, while diamond is covalent in all directions; (b) the secondary bonds between the chains, not the strong bonds along each chain.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 3: Reading a material from its bonding

Lessons 1–2 gave you the four bonds and their property packages. This lesson turns that into a **routine** for an unknown material, and asks you to **commit to a prediction before checking**.

### 1. The protocol

**Structure → bond → property package**, in that order:

1. **Describe the structure** in one sentence: what's attached to what, and how strongly?
2. **Name the bond:** metallic, ionic, covalent network, covalent molecular, or secondary.
3. **Write down the predictions:** conductive? bends or shatters? melting point?
4. **Then check** the answer.

**Write the prediction before you look.** If you peek first, it's easy to convince yourself you'd have got it right. A written prediction can be wrong, and finding out why is where you learn something. (It's the same idea as predict-then-measure in the factor of safety lesson.)

### 2. What the animation shows

| Step | Line | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Structure** | "4 unlike neighbours, one network" | each atom bonded to 4 others in a continuous 3D framework |
| **2. Bond** | covalent network | strong directional bonds; charge transport still needs a separate check |
| **3. Predict** | written **before** the reveal | hard, brittle, high-temperature resistance; conductivity to be checked |
| **4. Check** | silicon carbide | ✔ prediction holds |

Silicon carbide is used as abrasive grit and is a semiconductor. The mechanical predictions fit; the electrical exception is a reason to check rather than trust the bond label alone.

### 3. The worked example: a mystery white crystal

**Clues:** alternating + and − ions, shatters under a hammer, dissolves in water.

| Step | Reasoning |
|---|---|
| **Bond** | alternating ± ions → **ionic** |
| **Bends?** | no, sliding puts like charges together → **brittle** |
| **Conducts?** | no free electrons → **insulator as a solid** |
| **Melting** | strong charge attraction → **high** |
| **Bonus** | melted, the ions can move → **the melt conducts** |

**Reveal:** table salt. Every line checks out.

### 4. Why predictions go wrong: the usual causes

The page's main point is to **sort your mistakes by cause**, because the same few causes keep coming up:

| Mistake | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Called it a **network** when it's separate **molecules** | sulfur melts at only ~115 °C | ask: is it one big connected structure or many small pieces? |
| **Missed a second bond** | graphite conducts much better within its layers | check for weak bonds between layers or chains |
| **Forgot the melt** | molten salt conducts | ions move once melted |
| Assumed "strong bonds" = "strong material" | polyethylene has strong C–C bonds but is soft | the **weakest** bond (between chains) controls it |

Once your mistakes all sound like "I missed the second bond" instead of "I guessed," the method is working.

### 5. Preview: processing changes the structure

The bond package is the **starting point**. Processing changes the structure, and the properties follow:

| Process | What changes | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Cold working** (bending, hammering, rolling a metal) | creates tangles of defects | **harder**, less bendable. Bend a wire back and forth: it gets harder to bend permanently, then may crack; this is not a large increase in Young's modulus |
| **Quenching steel** (heat, then plunge in water or oil) | traps carbon in a strained structure | **much harder**, more brittle |
| **Stretching a plastic** | lines the chains up | **stronger along the stretch**. Pull a strip of plastic bag: it gets tougher as it thins |

Later lessons cover these in detail. The idea now: **properties come from structure, and processing changes structure.**

### 6. When to use this

- **Choosing a material** when you don't have test data yet
- **Troubleshooting a failure:** was this material ever suitable for the job? (A brittle ceramic may need a different geometry, support or impact rating. Bonding alone cannot certify whether an impact will break it.)

### 7. The method

1. One-sentence structure description.
2. Name the bond (or bonds).
3. Write the predictions: conduction, bend vs shatter, temperature behaviour.
4. Check.
5. For every miss, name the bond feature you overlooked.

**Try one.** Write your prediction for each before checking:

- (a) A shiny grey solid; atoms share a sea of loose electrons.
- (b) Long carbon chains, each strong internally, loosely attracted to each other.
- (c) Small ring-shaped molecules (8 atoms each), weakly attracted to each other.

Answers: (a) metallic: conducts, bends, moderate-to-high melting; (b) polymer, governed by secondary bonds: insulator, flexible, softens when warmed; (c) covalent molecular (this is sulfur): insulator, crumbly, low melting, because only the weak forces between molecules need to break.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 4: Crystal structures

The bond tells you *how* metal atoms stick together. This lesson covers **how they're arranged**, and why that arrangement decides whether a metal bends easily or cracks.

### 1. The unit cell: the repeating box

A metal crystal is a pattern repeated billions of times in every direction. You only need to understand **one box** of the pattern, called the **unit cell**. Stack it up and you have the whole crystal.

### 2. The three common metal structures

| Structure | Atom positions | Atoms per cell | Packing | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **BCC** (body-centred cubic) | 8 corners + 1 in the centre | **2** | 68% | iron (room temp), chromium, tungsten |
| **FCC** (face-centred cubic) | 8 corners + 1 on each of 6 faces | **4** | **74%** | aluminium, copper, nickel, stainless 304 |
| **HCP** (hexagonal close-packed) | hexagonal stacking | **6** in the conventional hexagonal cell (2 in a primitive cell) | **74%** | magnesium, titanium, zinc |

### 3. Counting atoms: sharing rules

Atoms on the edges of a box are **shared** with neighbouring boxes:

| Position | Shared by | Counts as |
|---|---|---|
| **Corner** | 8 boxes | ⅛ |
| **Face** | 2 boxes | ½ |
| **Centre** | just this box | 1 |

- **FCC:** 8 × ⅛ + 6 × ½ = 1 + 3 = **4**
- **BCC:** 8 × ⅛ + 1 = 1 + 1 = **2**

Get this count wrong and every number after it is wrong.

### 4. What the animation shows (aluminium density, from atoms)

Aluminium is FCC, with atom radius **143 pm** (1 pm = 10⁻¹² m).

| Step | What's done | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Cell face** | look at one face of the cube | |
| **2. Diagonal** | atoms **touch along the face diagonal**: 4 radii = diagonal = a√2 | edge a = 2√2 × 143 ≈ **404.5 pm** |
| **3. Count** | corners 1 + faces 3 | **4 atoms** |
| **4. Density** | mass of 4 atoms ÷ volume of box | **≈ 2.71 g/cm³** |

**The density step:**

- Mass of one atom = molar mass ÷ Avogadro's number = 26.98 ÷ 6.022×10²³ g
- Mass of the box = 4 × that ≈ 1.79×10⁻²² g
- Volume = a³ = (404.5×10⁻¹⁰ cm)³ ≈ 6.6×10⁻²³ cm³
- **Density ≈ 2.71 g/cm³**. The handbook says 2.70 ✔

You calculated a real material property from **atom size and arrangement**. If it had come out far off, the first suspect would be the wrong structure or radius, not the arithmetic.

### 5. Packing efficiency

Spheres can't fill space completely, so there's always some gap:

- Simple cubic: 52% (rarely used by metals)
- BCC: 68%
- **FCC and HCP: 74%**, the most possible for equal spheres

**Why it matters:** tightly packed layers are the **smoothest** sliding surfaces. Smoother sliding means it bends more easily, so it's more **ductile**.

### 6. Slip systems: why some metals bend and others crack

A **slip system** is a plane plus a direction along which atomic layers can slide.

| Structure | Slip systems | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **FCC** | 12, easy | almost always finds a way to bend → **very ductile**, even when cold |
| **BCC** | 12+, but they need heat to help | ductile when warm, can turn **brittle when very cold** |
| **HCP** | ~3 easy ones | limited ductility → **watch for cracking** |

**Real-world consequence:** ordinary carbon steel is BCC. In a hard prairie cold snap it can become **brittle**, so a hitch pin or bracket that would bend in summer might **snap** in winter. Aluminium and stainless 304 (FCC) don't have this problem. Cold-service toughness also depends on grade, processing, flaws, loading rate and section thickness; crystal structure alone is not a failure diagnosis.

### 7. Iron changes structure, and that's why steel heat treatment works

| Temperature | Iron is | Carbon dissolves? |
|---|---|---|
| Room temperature | **BCC** | barely |
| Above **912 °C** | **FCC** | readily (more room between atoms) |

**Heat treating steel (not a furnace recipe):**

912 °C is the transition for **pure iron**. Steel austenitizing temperatures depend on composition and the treatment specification.

1. Heat to the alloy-specific austenitizing range → FCC austenite forms → carbon dissolves in
2. **Quench** (plunge into water or oil) → it snaps back toward BCC too fast for the carbon to escape
3. Trapped carbon distorts the lattice → **very hard** (and more brittle)

That's how blacksmiths harden tools. It's a crystal structure change, put to use. (More on this in later lessons.)

### 8. The method

1. **Identify the structure:** FCC, BCC, or HCP.
2. **Count atoms per cell** with the sharing rules.
3. **Find the cell size** from where atoms touch: face diagonal for FCC, body diagonal for BCC.
4. **Density** = (atoms × atom mass) ÷ cell volume, then compare with the handbook.
5. **Predict deformation:** FCC forms easily; BCC watch the cold; HCP watch for cracking.

**Try one:** iron at room temperature is BCC with an atom radius of 124 pm (molar mass 55.85 g/mol). In BCC, atoms touch along the **body diagonal**: 4r = a√3.

- (a) What's the cell edge a?
- (b) How many atoms per cell?
- (c) What's the density?

You should get (a) about 286 pm, (b) 2, (c) about 7.9 g/cm³ (the handbook says 7.87).

## Materials 101 · Lesson 5: Grains, texture, and anisotropy

The last lesson treated a metal as one perfect crystal. Real metal is actually **many small crystals packed together**. This lesson shows why their **size** and **alignment** change strength.

### 1. Grains: a mosaic of crystals

A piece of steel is made of millions of tiny crystals called **grains**. Each one has its atoms neatly lined up, but **each grain points a different way**. Picture cobblestones: each stone is solid, and the joints between them run every which way.

The joints are **grain boundaries**, places where the neat pattern on one side doesn't line up with the next grain.

**Seeing them:** polish a steel sample and etch it with acid. The boundaries are messier, so they corrode faster and show up as **dark lines** under a microscope.

### 2. How metals bend: dislocations

Metals don't bend by sliding whole planes of atoms at once. That would take enormous force. They bend by moving small **defects** called **dislocations**, one row of atoms at a time.

**Rug analogy:** dragging a big rug is hard. Pushing a **ruck** (wrinkle) across it is easy, and the rug still moves. A dislocation is that ruck.

### 3. Grain boundaries block dislocations

When a dislocation reaches a grain boundary, the next grain's atoms point a different way, so its motion is **impeded or redirected**. Dislocations **pile up** against the boundary.

**Smaller grains → more boundaries → more walls → harder to bend → stronger.**

### 4. Hall–Petch: how much stronger?

**σy = σ₀ + k ÷ √d**

- **σy** = yield strength
- **σ₀** = the base strength
- **k** = how effective the boundaries are
- **d** = grain diameter

Smaller d → bigger k/√d → stronger.

### 5. What the animation shows

The same steel, two grain sizes (σ₀ = 100 MPa, k = 0.50 MPa·√m):

| Step | Grain size | √d | Yield strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Coarse** | 100 μm | 0.01 | 100 + 0.50/0.01 = **150 MPa** |
| **2. Refine** | 25 μm | 0.005 | 100 + 0.50/0.005 = **200 MPa** |
| **3–4. Result** | | | **+50 MPa (+33%)** |

**Same chemistry, 33% stronger**, just from making the grains smaller. Grain size is a real property, so it belongs on a drawing or spec alongside the alloy name.

(1 μm = 1 micrometre = 0.001 mm. A human hair is about 70 μm.)

### 6. Limits: finer isn't always better

- **Extremely tiny grains** (below ~20 nanometres): grains start sliding past each other instead of blocking, so the metal can actually get weaker.
- **At high temperature:** boundaries become **weak paths**. Grains can slide along them slowly under load (**creep**). Hot parts like turbine blades often use **large** grains, or even a single crystal, for this reason.
- **Corrosion:** boundaries corrode faster and can be where cracks start.

So: **fine grains for room-temperature strength; coarse grains for high-temperature creep resistance.**

### 7. Texture and anisotropy: direction matters

**Rolling, forging and drawing** push grains to line up in a preferred direction. That's **texture**, and it makes properties **depend on direction**, which is called **anisotropy**.

Think of **wood grain**: strong along it, splits easily across it. Processed metal is a milder version of the same thing.

**Where you see it:**

- **Rolled sheet** can have direction-dependent properties; use the measured orientation for the grade. When **bending sheet metal**, bend lines running **across** the rolling direction are less likely to crack than bends along it.
- **Forged wrenches and crankshafts** are strong because the grain flow follows the part's shape. A part **machined** from bar cuts across the grain flow.
- **Deep-drawn cups** can develop wavy rims ("ears") where the texture is uneven.

**It isn't a defect.** It's a record of how the metal was made. Either **design with it** (line up the strong direction with the load) or **remove it** with cross-rolling or annealing. Just don't assume the metal is the same in every direction.

### 8. The method

1. **Grain size changed?** Use Hall–Petch to predict the new yield strength.
2. **Which way does the load run** relative to the rolling or forging direction? With the texture or against it?
3. **Hot service?** Ask whether the boundaries are helping (room-temperature strength) or hurting (creep).
4. **Be able to point at a part** and say: strong this way, weak that way, because of how it was made.

**Try one:** using the same steel (σ₀ = 100 MPa, k = 0.50 MPa·√m):

- (a) What's the yield strength with 50 μm grains?
- (b) With 10 μm grains?

Hint: convert μm to m first (50 μm = 50×10⁻⁶ m), then take the square root. You should get (a) about 171 MPa and (b) about 258 MPa.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 6: Amorphous solids and what microscopes show

The last two lessons were about crystals: atoms in neat repeating patterns. This lesson covers solids with **no long-range repeating pattern**, and how to **read a broken part** to work out how it failed.

### 1. Crystal vs amorphous

| | Crystal | Amorphous (glass) |
|---|---|---|
| Atom arrangement | neat, repeating rows | jumbled, no long-range pattern |
| Like… | oranges stacked in a crate | marbles poured into a jar and frozen in place |
| Examples | steel, aluminium, salt | window glass, many plastics, metallic glass |

"Glass" here means **any** solid with frozen disorder, not just window glass.

### 2. Melting vs the glass transition

| | Crystal | Glass |
|---|---|---|
| Softens | at **one** sharp temperature (ice melts at exactly 0 °C) | **gradually**, over a range |
| Why | the neat pattern collapses all at once | there's no pattern to collapse; atoms just get gradually more mobile |
| Name | melting point, Tm | **glass transition, Tg** |

Below Tg the atoms are frozen mid-shuffle. Above it, the amorphous regions become more mobile and often rubbery; this is not automatically melting or free flow. Crystals and crosslinks can still support the material.

**Myth check:** old church windows thicker at the bottom aren't from glass flowing over centuries. At room temperature it's far too stiff. They were made uneven.

### 3. Cooling rate decides crystal vs glass

Every liquid **wants** to crystallize. It just needs time for the atoms to find their spots.

- **Cool slowly** → atoms have time to line up → **crystal**
- **Cool very fast** → atoms get frozen in place → **glass**

Window glass crystallizes sluggishly, so ordinary cooling is fast enough. Metals crystallize **eagerly**, so a **metallic glass** needs cooling at thousands to millions of degrees per second, or a special multi-element alloy that "confuses" the atoms.

That's processing → structure again, as in lesson 3.

### 4. What the animation shows: metallic glass vs high-strength steel

| | Steel | Metallic glass |
|---|---|---|
| Stays springy (elastic) up to | 1.5 GPa at **0.75%** stretch | 1.9 GPa at **2%** stretch |
| Spring energy stored (σ² ÷ 2E) | 5.6 MJ/m³ | **19 MJ/m³** |

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Spring** | both stretch elastically; the glass goes much further |
| **2. Energy** | the glass stores **3–4× more** spring energy |
| **3. Shear band** | push the glass past 2% and all the deformation concentrates in **one thin band** |
| **4. Yield** | steel bends and gives warning; the glass **fails suddenly** |

**Why?** With no crystal pattern, there are **no dislocations** (lesson 5's "rug wrinkles"). It can't bend gradually. Once it gives, it all goes at once along one band.

**Good for:** springs, blade edges, golf club faces, where you want high springiness and it never needs to bend.
**Bad for:** a bracket or anything that should **bend and warn you** before breaking.

### 5. The microscope ladder

| Tool | Sees down to | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Optical** (with polish and etch) | ~1 μm | grains and phases (the mosaic from lesson 5) |
| **SEM** (scanning electron) | ~10 nm | fracture surfaces: dimples, fatigue lines, inclusions |
| **TEM** (transmission electron) | atoms | individual dislocations, atomic columns |

A failure investigation climbs this ladder: optical first, SEM for the break surface, TEM only if the underlying mechanism is in question.

### 6. Reading a fracture surface

Look at a broken part before you blame the load:

| What the break looks like | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Dull, grey, fibrous; stretched or necked** (under SEM: **dimples**) | **ductile overload**: it stretched before it parted. A bolt pulled apart typically shows a "cup and cone." |
| **Flat, bright, sparkly or grainy facets** | **brittle**: it cracked along crystal planes. Common in cold steel (lesson 4) or hardened parts. |
| **Mirror, mist and hackle regions** | often glass fracture; river patterns are commonly associated with crystalline cleavage |
| **Smooth zone with curved "beach marks," then a rough final zone** | **fatigue**: a crack grew slowly over many cycles, then the rest snapped |

### 7. The method

1. **Crystal or amorphous?** Glass softens over a range (Tg); a crystal melts sharply (Tm).
2. **Metallic glass:** very springy and hard, but fails suddenly. Check the specific alloy, geometry and failure behaviour where warning before failure matters.
3. **Broken part:** read the surface first. Dimples or fibrous means ductile; flat facets means brittle; beach marks mean fatigue.

**Try one:** identify the failure from each description.

- (a) A snapped trailer pin with a flat, sparkly break, found on a −35 °C morning.
- (b) A pulled-apart bolt that narrowed before breaking, with a dull, fibrous cup-shaped end.
- (c) A shaft with smooth curved bands across most of the break and a small rough patch at one edge.

Answers: (a) brittle fracture, likely BCC steel going brittle in the cold; (b) ductile overload; (c) fatigue: a crack grew over many cycles, and the rough patch is where it finally snapped.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 7: Defects are the material

So far, defects (dislocations, grain boundaries) have been mentioned in passing. This lesson argues they aren't flaws to minimise. **They're what make metals behave the way they do.**

### 1. The puzzle: real metals are far too weak

If you calculate how hard it should be to slide one **perfect** plane of atoms over another, all at once:

- Simple estimate for iron: **~13 GPa**
- Refined estimate: **~2.7 GPa**
- Real iron yields at: **10–1,000× lower** than that

The theory isn't slightly off. It's describing the wrong mechanism.

### 2. The answer: dislocations

Real crystals deform by moving **dislocations**, defects that let atoms shift **one row at a time**. It's the **rug wrinkle** from lesson 5: you don't drag the whole rug, you push a ruck across it. Much less force does the same job.

**Strength is about how hard it is for dislocations to move.** Block them and the metal gets stronger. Let them glide freely and it's soft.

### 3. The three defect families (by dimension)

| Dimension | Type | What it is | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0D** (a point) | **vacancy** | a missing atom | lets atoms hop around (diffusion) |
| | **interstitial** | an extra atom squeezed between others | **carbon in iron**, which is what makes steel |
| **1D** (a line) | **dislocation** | the edge of an extra half-plane of atoms | lets metal bend; tangled ones = strength |
| **2D** (a surface) | **grain boundary**, stacking fault | where crystal orientation changes | blocks dislocations (Hall–Petch, lesson 5) |

### 4. Vacancies and temperature

Some atom sites are always empty, and how many depends **hugely** on temperature:

**n/N = exp(−Q ÷ kT)**

- **n/N** = fraction of sites that are empty
- **Q** = energy to make one vacancy (copper ≈ 0.9 eV)
- **k** = Boltzmann's constant = 8.617×10⁻⁵ eV/K
- **T** = temperature in **kelvin**
- **exp(x)** means e^x, with e ≈ 2.718

**Step by step at 1,000 K:**

1. kT = 8.617×10⁻⁵ × 1,000 = 0.0862 eV
2. Q ÷ kT = 0.9 ÷ 0.0862 = 10.44
3. exp(−10.44) ≈ **2.9×10⁻⁵**, so about 3 in every 100,000 sites are empty

**At 300 K (room temperature):** Q/kT = 34.8 → exp(−34.8) ≈ **7.6×10⁻¹⁶**, or about 1 in a million billion

### 5. What the animation shows

| Step | Temperature | Empty fraction |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Room temp** | 300 K | 7.6×10⁻¹⁶ |
| **2. Heat** | rising | climbs steeply |
| **3. Ten orders** | 1,000 K | 2.9×10⁻⁵ |

**700 K of heating multiplied the vacancies by about 40 billion.** The exponential makes temperature a powerful dial.

**Quenching** (cooling very fast) **freezes** that high-temperature vacancy count into the cold metal. Those extra vacancies then help atoms move around inside, which drives aging and precipitation hardening (coming in later lessons).

### 6. Two ways to make a metal stronger

Both come down to **blocking dislocations**:

| Method | How it blocks them |
|---|---|
| **Fine grains** | more boundaries = more walls (Hall–Petch) |
| **Cold work** (bending, rolling, hammering cold) | creates so many dislocations they **tangle** and jam each other |

### 7. Processing steps and the defect they target

| Process | Effect on defects | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Quench** | freezes vacancies in | sets up later hardening |
| **Cold work** | multiplies dislocations | **harder**, less ductile |
| **Anneal** (heat and hold, cool slowly) | sweeps out vacancies and dislocations | **softer**, more ductile again |

**A shop example:** bend copper tubing back and forth and it gets **harder to bend permanently** (cold work, tangled dislocations), without a comparable increase in elastic modulus. A controlled anneal can restore ductility. For a real component, follow the alloy and component specification rather than treating this as a torch-heating procedure. That's why plumbers and HVAC techs anneal copper before flaring or bending.

### 8. The method

1. **Why is this metal soft or strong?** Look at its **defects**, not just its composition.
2. **Classify:** point (0D), line (1D), or surface (2D).
3. **Vacancies:** n/N = exp(−Q/kT). Temperature has a huge effect.
4. **Strength** = how blocked the dislocations are.
5. **Name the process → defect link:** quench, cold work, anneal.

**Try one:**

- (a) What fraction of copper's sites are empty at 600 K? (Q = 0.9 eV)
- (b) A steel wire has been bent so many times it's stiff and about to crack. What process restores it, and which defects does it remove?

You should get (a) about 2.7×10⁻⁸; (b) annealing, which removes the tangled dislocations.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 8: Atoms move downhill

The last lesson showed that vacancies let atoms hop around inside a solid. This lesson covers **how fast** they spread, and how to calculate furnace time for a heat treatment like **case hardening**.

### 1. Diffusion: atoms spreading out

Put a drop of food colouring in still water and it slowly spreads until the colour is even. Atoms in a hot solid do the same thing, just much more slowly. Substitutional atoms can hop into vacancies; small atoms such as carbon in iron move between interstitial sites. In this simple concentration-driven model, the net flow goes from **higher to lower concentration**.

### 2. Carburizing: the real-world reason this matters

Low-carbon steel is **tough** but **soft**. To make a gear or pin with a **hard, wear-resistant surface** but a **tough core**:

1. Heat it (here 950 °C) in a carbon-rich atmosphere.
2. Carbon **diffuses in** from the surface.
3. Quench: the high-carbon outer layer (the **case**) becomes hard, and the low-carbon core stays tough.

(The "case hardening" compounds sold for shop use do the same thing on a small scale.)

### 3. Fick's first law: flow goes downhill

**J = −D × (dC/dx)**

- **J** = diffusion flux: amount crossing **unit area per second** (for example atoms/(m²·s), with a compatible concentration scale)
- **D** = diffusion coefficient (how easily atoms move)
- **dC/dx** = how steeply the concentration changes with depth

**The key point: it's the slope that matters, not the amount.** No difference in concentration means no flow, however much carbon there is. The **minus sign** means flow goes from high to low, downhill.

### 4. Diffusion depth grows with √time

The profile spreads over a distance of about **2√(Dt)**:

| Want this depth | Takes this long |
|---|---|
| 1× | 1× |
| **2×** | **4×** |
| 10× | **100×** |

That's why carburized cases are **millimetres**, not centimetres. Going deeper gets expensive fast.

### 5. Temperature has a big effect: Arrhenius

**D = D₀ × exp(−Q ÷ RT)**

The same exponential form as the vacancy formula in lesson 7. **T must be in kelvin** (950 °C = 1,223 K).

For carbon in hot steel, a **50 °C rise roughly doubles D**, which roughly halves the time.

### 6. What the animation shows (carburizing 4 hours at 950 °C)

The surface is held at 1.1% carbon, and the steel starts at 0.2%. **How deep does 0.4% carbon reach?**

| Step | What's calculated | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Surface** | D = 2.3×10⁻⁵ × exp(−148,000 ÷ (8.314 × 1,223)) | D ≈ **1.1×10⁻¹¹ m²/s** |
| **2. Diffuse** | 2√(D × 14,400 s) | length scale ≈ **0.80 mm** |
| **3. Case depth** | how far until carbon drops to 0.4% | **≈ 0.69 mm** |

**The case-depth step:**

- Fraction of the way from surface to core: (1.1 − 0.4) ÷ (1.1 − 0.2) = 0.778
- Look up the "inverse error function" of 0.778 → **0.86** (a table or calculator lookup, like looking up a sine)
- Depth = 0.86 × 0.80 mm ≈ **0.69 mm**

The curve in the animation drops from 1.1% at the surface toward 0.2% deep inside. The "case edge" is where it crosses 0.4%.

### 7. The time–temperature trade

| Temperature | D | Time for the same 0.69 mm case |
|---|---|---|
| 900 °C | 5.9×10⁻¹² | **7.4 h** |
| 950 °C | 1.1×10⁻¹¹ | **4 h** |

Hotter = faster, but it costs more energy and can coarsen the grains (lesson 5). Keep **D × t** the same and you can trade one for the other.

### 8. Unit check

D is in **m²/s**, so D × t is m², and √(Dt) is **metres** ✔. If √(Dt) doesn't come out as a length, something's set up wrong.

### 9. The method

1. **Convert to kelvin** and seconds.
2. **D = D₀ exp(−Q/RT).**
3. **Length scale = 2√(Dt).**
4. **For a specific concentration:** find the fraction, look up the inverse error function, and multiply.
5. **Remember the scaling:** double the depth = 4× the time; +50 °C ≈ half the time.

**Try one:**

- (a) At 950 °C, how long for a **1.0 mm** case instead of 0.69 mm? (Use the √t scaling.)
- (b) At **1,000 °C**, D ≈ 1.95×10⁻¹¹ m²/s. How long for the original 0.69 mm case?

You should get (a) about 8.4 h, since (1.0 ÷ 0.69)² × 4; (b) about 2.3 h, since 4 × (1.1 ÷ 1.95).

## Materials 101 · Lesson 9: Processing is defect engineering

This lesson pulls together crystals (lesson 4), defects (lesson 7) and diffusion (lesson 8) into the shop's main tool for changing steel: **heat treatment**. The same steel with a different heating history becomes a completely different material.

### 1. Same steel, three histories

All three bars are **4140 steel**, with identical chemistry:

| Treatment | Hardness | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Annealed** | ~200 HB | soft, easy to machine, bends |
| **Quenched** from 850 °C | ~58 HRC | very hard (file-hard, scratches glass), **brittle**, snaps |
| **Quenched + tempered** at 400 °C | ~42 HRC | hard **and** tough, the working compromise |

*(HB = Brinell, HRC = Rockwell C. Both are hardness scales. For reference, good knife blades are around 58–62 HRC.)*

**Nothing about the composition changed.** Only the defects and structure inside moved.

### 2. What the animation shows

| Step | Path | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Anneal** | heat, cool slowly | soft baseline |
| **2. Quench** | heat to 850 °C, plunge into water or oil | very hard, brittle |
| **3. Temper** | after quenching, reheat to 400 °C | gives up some hardness for toughness |
| **4. Compare** | | machinable → glass-scratching → usable |

### 3. Annealing: healing

Heat it up and hold it, and three things happen in order:

1. **Recovery:** dislocations rearrange, and some cancel out. Internal stress drops.
2. **Recrystallization:** brand-new, defect-free grains form.
3. **Grain growth:** those grains get bigger.

Result: usually **softer and more ductile**, with reduced residual stress—not a guarantee of zero stress.

**The catch:** anneal too long or too hot and the grains get **too big**. Hall–Petch (lesson 5) says big grains mean weak metal. The page calls annealing "controlled forgetting": forget too much and you lose strength you wanted to keep.

### 4. Quenching: freezing

1. Heat steel above ~850 °C → iron becomes FCC (**austenite**), and carbon dissolves in (lesson 4).
2. Cool it **so fast that carbon has no time to diffuse out** (lesson 8).
3. The iron snaps toward BCC anyway, but the trapped carbon **distorts** it into **martensite**: a strained, very hard, brittle structure.

**Quench severity** is a dial:

| Quench | Speed | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Water / brine | fastest | **quench cracks** (thermal stress beats the fresh martensite's strength) |
| Oil | medium | less cracking |
| Air | slowest | only works on certain alloy steels |

### 5. Tempering: toughening

Fresh martensite is too brittle to use. **Reheat it gently** (well below the hardening temperature) and some carbon can move slightly, relieving the strain.

- **Lower temper** → keeps more hardness (cutting edges)
- **Higher temper** → more toughness (springs, structural parts)

Blacksmiths judge tempering by **oxide colours** on polished steel: straw yellow (~220 °C) for hard edges, through bronze and purple to **blue** (~300 °C) for springs. The page's 400 °C is hotter than that, aiming for toughness.

### 6. Case hardening: changing only the skin

These treatments **do** change composition near the surface:

- **Carburizing:** carbon diffuses in (lesson 8's 0.69 mm case), then quench → **hard skin, tough core**. Used for gears and pins.
- **Nitriding:** nitrogen instead of carbon, at lower temperature, with **no quench needed**, so less distortion.

The case depth on a drawing is a diffusion length with a tolerance.

### 7. The TTT diagram

A **time–temperature–transformation** chart shows how long steel takes to transform at each temperature. It has a **"nose"**, the point where transformation happens **fastest**.

A TTT chart describes isothermal holds; a continuous-cooling transformation (CCT) chart is the appropriate map for a changing cooling path. The teaching idea is to avoid substantial diffusional transformation before reaching the martensite range. That's what "quench fast enough" means in practice.

### 8. The method

For any heat-treatment step, name **the defect move** and **the property that changes**:

| Step | Defect move | Property change |
|---|---|---|
| Anneal | removes dislocations, grows grains | softer, ductile |
| Quench | traps carbon (martensite) | much harder, brittle |
| Temper | lets trapped carbon relax a little | tougher, a bit less hard |
| Carburize / nitride | adds carbon or nitrogen to the skin | hard surface, tough core |

**Try one:** which treatment fixes each problem?

- (a) A freshly hardened chisel chips at the edge when struck.
- (b) You need to drill holes in a part that's already been hardened.
- (c) A gear's teeth wear quickly, but the gear also has to survive shock loads.
- (d) A welded frame has built-in stress that's causing distortion.

Answers: (a) temper it, or temper at a higher temperature, for more toughness; (b) anneal to soften it, machine it, then re-harden; (c) case harden by carburizing or nitriding, for a hard skin on a tough core; (d) stress-relief anneal, which is the recovery stage.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 10: Reading a stress-strain curve

This lesson is about the most important graph in materials: **the tensile test curve**. Each part of the curve is a different property, and the main skill is not mixing them up.

### 1. The test

A machine grips a bar and **pulls it until it breaks**, recording:

- **Stress** σ = force ÷ **original** area (lesson 19 of physics)
- **Strain** ε = stretch ÷ **original** length

Plot stress (up) vs strain (across). That curve is the material's "mechanical autobiography."

### 2. The four landmarks

| Landmark | Where on the curve | What it tells you | Units |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Slope** | the steep straight start | **stiffness, E**: how much it stretches elastically | GPa |
| **2. Yield** | where it bends away from straight | **yield strength, σy**: where permanent bending starts | MPa |
| **3. Peak** | the top of the curve | **ultimate tensile strength, σuts**: the most it ever carried | MPa |
| **4. Fracture** | where the line ends | **ductility**: how far it stretched before breaking | % or a ratio |

### 3. What the animation shows

| Step | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Slope** | E = 200 GPa | steel: stiff, barely stretches elastically |
| **2. Yield** | 350 MPa | below this it springs back; above, it stays bent |
| **3. Peak** | 420 MPa | max stress; after this the bar starts to **neck** (thin down in one spot) |
| **4. Fracture** | end of curve | it breaks |

**Why does the curve go down after the peak?** After necking, the measured load falls while engineering stress still divides by the fixed **original** area. Local true stress may continue rising as the neck thins; damage can later reverse that trend.

### 4. The worked example: 1020 steel bar

12.5 mm diameter, so area = π/4 × 12.5² = **122.7 mm²**.

| Reading | Load | Stress |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves the straight line | 42.9 kN | 42,900 ÷ 122.7 = **350 MPa** (yield) |
| Peak load | 51.5 kN | 51,500 ÷ 122.7 = **420 MPa** (ultimate) |
| Initial slope | | **200 GPa** (stiffness) |

Three numbers, three different properties:

- **350** = where it stopped springing back
- **420** = the most it ever carried
- **200 GPa** = how little it stretched getting there

### 5. The 0.2% offset: a standard yield point

Many alloys (aluminium, stainless) **bend gradually** with no sharp corner, so "where it leaves the line" is guesswork.

**The convention:**

1. Start at strain **0.002** (0.2%) on the horizontal axis.
2. Draw a line **parallel to the elastic slope**.
3. Where it crosses the curve = **yield strength**.

It's an arbitrary choice, like a speed limit. What matters is that **everyone measures it the same way**, so datasheets are comparable.

### 6. Stiffness ≠ strength ≠ hardness

The most common mix-up in the subject:

| Word | What it means | How it's measured |
|---|---|---|
| **Stiffness** (E) | resists **stretching** elastically | the **slope** of the curve |
| **Strength** (σy, σuts) | resists **permanent bending or breaking** | **heights** on the curve |
| **Hardness** | resists **surface denting or scratching** | a separate indentation test |

**Example:** glass is **stiff** (E ≈ 70 GPa) and can be quite **strong**, but it's brittle, the opposite of tough. Don't say "strong" when you mean stiff, or "hard" when you mean strong.

(Hardness does roughly track strength **for steels**, which is why shops use a quick hardness test to estimate strength. It doesn't hold for most other materials.)

### 7. Check the axes first

Strain scales vary hugely between materials:

| Material | Whole curve fits in… |
|---|---|
| Ceramic | below 0.002 (0.2%) |
| Steel | ~0.2–0.4 |
| Rubber or polymer | past 2 (200%+) |

Put them on the same axes and one becomes invisible. **Always read the axis limits before judging the shape.**

**A straight line all the way to fracture means brittle.** It never yielded at all.

### 8. The method

1. **Slope** → E (GPa)
2. **0.2% offset** → yield σy (MPa)
3. **Peak** → ultimate σuts (MPa)
4. **End point** → ductility (strain at fracture)
5. **Name each one separately.** Don't just say "strong."

**Try one:** a 10 mm aluminium bar (E = 69 GPa) is tested. The 0.2% offset yield occurs at 21.2 kN and the peak load is 24.4 kN.

- (a) What's the area?
- (b) What are the yield and ultimate strengths?
- (c) About what strain is it at when it first yields (elastic part only)?

You should get (a) 78.5 mm², (b) about 270 MPa yield and 311 MPa ultimate, (c) about 270 ÷ 69,000 ≈ 0.004.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 11: Ductility and toughness

The last lesson read **heights** on the stress-strain curve (yield, ultimate). This one reads its **width** and **area**, which tell you how much energy a material can absorb before breaking. The answer often surprises people: **the "stronger" material can break first.**

### 1. Three different things

| Property | On the curve | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Strength** | **height** | how much stress it can take |
| **Ductility** | **width** | how far it stretches before breaking |
| **Toughness** | **area underneath** | how much **energy** it absorbs before breaking |

**Why area = energy:** stress (force ÷ area) × strain (stretch ÷ length) = energy ÷ volume. MPa × strain gives **MJ/m³**.

### 2. What the animation shows

Two bars, same size, with a weight dropped on each:

| | High-strength steel | Mild steel |
|---|---|---|
| Max stress | **1,500 MPa** | 350–420 MPa |
| Stretches to | **3%** | **36%** |
| Curve shape | tall, thin **spike** | long, broad **curve** |
| Energy absorbed | ≈ **40 MJ/m³** | ≈ **140 MJ/m³** |

**The "weaker" mild steel absorbs over 3× more energy.** For these illustrative curves and equal material volume, the high-strength bar has the lower tensile energy capacity. A real impact also depends on geometry, notches, loading rate and temperature.

**Quick estimate:** area ≈ average stress × strain at fracture.

- Mild: ~390 × 0.36 ≈ **140** ✔
- High-strength: ~1,350 × 0.03 ≈ **40** ✔

### 3. Why this matters

Anything that must **absorb a hit or fail gracefully** should be chosen by **area**, not peak:

- **Rollover protection (ROPS) and crash structures:** designed to bend and soak up energy, not snap
- **Tow chains and straps:** stretch before breaking
- **Seismic building frames:** bend, don't shatter

A ceramic's whole curve is a tiny spike (~0.1 MJ/m³), about **1,000× less** than mild steel. That's why a ceramic insert shatters when dropped.

### 4. Two ways to measure ductility

**Elongation** (%EL): how much longer the test section got.

- 50 mm section → 68 mm after breaking: (68 − 50) ÷ 50 = **36%**

**Reduction of area** (%RA): how much the neck thinned.

- 10.0 mm bar (78.5 mm²) → neck 6.3 mm (31.2 mm²): (78.5 − 31.2) ÷ 78.5 ≈ **60%**

Both answer "how much did it flow before it separated?" %RA is more reliable when the test length varies.

**Ductility isn't fixed.** Cold temperature (lesson 4's BCC steel) or a **notch** (a sharp groove or crack) can make a normally ductile metal break brittle.

### 5. The strength-ductility trade-off

**The page's worked example:** the same 1020 steel, annealed vs cold-worked:

| | Annealed | Cold-worked |
|---|---|---|
| Yield | 350 MPa | **700 MPa** (2×) |
| Elongation | 36% | **8%** |
| Toughness | 141 MJ/m³ | **57 MJ/m³** (less than half) |

Cold work **doubled the strength** but used up most of the stretch. Area went **down** even though height went **up**.

**Why the trade-off exists:** most strengthening methods (cold work, alloying, precipitation) work by **blocking dislocations** (lesson 7). But **bending *is* dislocations moving**. Block them and you raise strength **and** cut how far the metal can stretch. It's the same mechanism working both ways.

**The exception:** **grain refinement** (lesson 5) usually raises strength **and** toughness together, which is why it's so valued.

### 6. The method

1. **Ductility:** read %EL (or %RA).
2. **Toughness:** estimate the area. Average stress × fracture strain is a good quick estimate.
3. **For impact or crash jobs:** compare candidates by **area**, not peak strength.
4. **Remember:** most strengthening costs ductility. Grain refinement is the exception.

**Try one:**

- (a) An aluminium alloy yields at 270 MPa, peaks at 310 MPa, and breaks at 12% elongation. Estimate its toughness.
- (b) A 50 mm gauge length measures 56 mm after fracture. What's the %EL?
- (c) A 10 mm bar necks down to 7.5 mm. What's the %RA?

You should get (a) about 290 × 0.12 ≈ 35 MJ/m³, (b) 12%, (c) about 44%.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 12: From curve to design allowable

This lesson connects the tensile curve (lesson 10) to the factor of safety (physics lesson 21). It answers the question **"What number am I actually allowed to design to?"** That number is a lot lower than the curve's headline value.

### 1. Real data is noisy

Real tests aren't clean textbook curves. The load cell hums, the stretch gauge slips slightly, and the start of the curve wobbles. **Three bars from the same batch** might read 342, 358 and 349 MPa.

So you need a **defined method**, not eyesight:

- **Stiffness E:** fit a best-fit straight line through the early, straight part. Stop where the points start curving away.
- **Yield:** let the 0.2% offset line (lesson 10) find the crossing.
- Use the test method, instrument calibration and specimen repeatability to estimate uncertainty; a universal 1% error cannot be promised.

### 2. Engineering vs true stress

| | Divides force by… | After necking |
|---|---|---|
| **Engineering stress** | **original** area | curve **falls** |
| **True stress** | **actual** (shrinking) area | keeps **rising** |

The fall in engineering stress reflects a falling measured load, using a fixed original area. The neck's area is shrinking faster than the metal is hardening.

**Converting** (valid up to necking):

- True stress = eng. stress × (1 + eng. strain)
- True strain = ln(1 + eng. strain)

**Example:** at 400 MPa and 0.20 strain: true stress = 400 × 1.20 = **480 MPa**, true strain = ln 1.20 ≈ **0.18**

**For these small-deformation classroom checks, use engineering stress** with compatible allowables. Large-deformation models may require true stress and strain.

### 3. Scatter → characteristic value

Every bar is a bit different. You design to a value **near the low end** of the spread, not the average.

**What the animation shows (titanium alloy Ti-6Al-4V):**

| Step | What's calculated | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Specimens** | three yield readings | 872, 879, 885 MPa |
| **2. Mean − 2s** | mean 879, spread s ≈ 6.5 | 879 − 2 × 6.5 = **866 MPa** (characteristic) |
| **3. Safety factor** | ÷ 1.5 | **577 MPa** (allowable) |
| **4. The gap** | the curve said ~880; design uses 577 | |

**Standard deviation (s)** measures the spread. Here's how to work it out:

1. Mean = (872 + 879 + 885) ÷ 3 ≈ 879
2. Each value's distance from the mean, squared: about 44, 0, 40
3. Add them (≈ 85), divide by (n − 1) = 2 → 42
4. Square root → **s ≈ 6.5 MPa**

**Mean − 2s is only a classroom screening estimate.** With three specimens it does not establish a certified lower bound or a stated population coverage. Real allowables need the applicable sampling and tolerance-bound method.

### 4. Allowable = characteristic ÷ factor of safety

The factor of safety covers what the scatter doesn't (physics lesson 21):

- uncertainty in the **loads**
- simplifications in the **analysis**
- how bad **failure** would be

A footbridge and a spacecraft bracket won't use the same factor.

### 5. The allowable belongs to the material and the process

Heat treatment moves the whole curve (lesson 9): quench-and-temper raises it, annealing lowers it. So **"4140 steel" alone isn't enough** to set an allowable. It has to be **"4140, quenched and tempered at X."** The number belongs to the material **and** its processing history.

### 6. The method

1. **Extract** E (fitted slope), σy (0.2% offset), σuts (peak) from each test.
2. **Find the spread** across specimens: mean and s.
3. **Characteristic** ≈ mean − 2s.
4. **Allowable** = characteristic ÷ factor of safety.
5. **Write it out** with every source named: "Allowable 390 MPa = 585 MPa characteristic ÷ 1.5."

**Try one:**

- (a) The three 1020 steel bars from the top of the page read 342, 358 and 349 MPa. Find the mean, s, the characteristic value, and the allowable with a factor of safety of 2.
- (b) A test bar is at 420 MPa engineering stress and 0.25 strain, just before necking. What are the true stress and true strain?

You should get (a) mean ≈ 350, s ≈ 8.0, characteristic ≈ 334 MPa, allowable ≈ 167 MPa; (b) true stress = 525 MPa, true strain ≈ 0.22.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 13: The four ways to make a metal strong

Earlier lessons kept coming back to one idea: **strength = how hard it is to move dislocations**. This lesson lays out the **four ways** to block them, and what each one costs.

### 1. The big picture

Metals bend by moving dislocations, the rug wrinkles from lesson 5. **Every strengthening method puts obstacles in their way.** The four methods just use different obstacles.

### 2. The four mechanisms

| Mechanism | Obstacle | Gain | Price | Real example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Grain refinement** | grain boundaries (walls) | good | **ductility barely affected**; lost if grains grow during heating | controlled-rolled structural plate |
| **Work hardening** (cold work) | tangled dislocations jamming each other | big | **ductility collapses**; becomes direction-dependent | cold-drawn rod, copper tube after bending |
| **Solid solution** | foreign atoms distorting the lattice | moderate | costs alloy money; **diminishing returns** | brass (zinc in copper), alloy steels |
| **Precipitation** | tiny particles dislocations must bend around | **biggest** | needs precise furnace timing; **overaging** ruins it | 6061-**T6** aluminium, many aerospace alloys |

### 3. What the animation shows

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Glide** | a dislocation moves freely along a slip plane: soft metal |
| **2. Obstacles** | each of the four obstacles appears and blocks it |
| **3. Price** | each one's cost: ductility, alloy cost, or furnace discipline |

### 4. Grain refinement (Hall–Petch again)

**Supplied classroom model for 1045 steel:** σ₀ = 110 MPa, k = **0.65 MPa·√m**, with d entered in metres:

| Grain size | Calculation | Yield |
|---|---|---|
| 20 μm | 110 + 0.65 ÷ √(20×10⁻⁶) = 110 + 145 | **255 MPa** |
| 5 μm | 110 + 0.65 ÷ √(5×10⁻⁶) = 110 + 291 | **401 MPa** |

**57% stronger, with the same chemistry.** A dislocation now hits a wall every 5 μm instead of every 20.

This is the **only** mechanism that raises strength **without** sacrificing much ductility. The catch is that heating can make grains grow back and undo it.

### 5. Work hardening

Bend or roll the metal cold and dislocations multiply until they **tangle like a forest**, blocking each other.

- ✔ big strength gain, and free if you're forming the part anyway
- ✘ **ductility collapses** (lesson 11: 36% → 8% elongation)
- ✘ properties become direction-dependent

Bend a wire back and forth: it resists further permanent bending more strongly, but accumulating damage can make it crack. Elastic stiffness is a different property.

### 6. Solid solution

Mix in atoms of a different size. They **distort** the crystal and snag passing dislocations.

**Diminishing returns:** the gain grows with **√(amount)**:

| Solute | Gain |
|---|---|
| 1% | 1× |
| 2% | √2 ≈ **1.41×** (not 2×) |
| 4% | **2×** |

Doubling the alloying doesn't double the benefit.

### 7. Precipitation hardening

Heat-treat to grow **tiny particles** inside the metal. Dislocations have to **bow around them**, like a rope wrapped around fence posts. That takes a lot of force.

- ✔ the **biggest** strength gain
- ✘ needs exact time and temperature
- ✘ **overaging:** too long or too hot and the particles grow large and far apart, so strength **drops**

**Practical example:** **6061-T6** aluminium. "T6" means it's been heat-treated for precipitation hardening. **Welding** it heats the area around the weld and **overages** it, so the heat-affected zone can lose a big chunk of strength. That's why welded aluminium structures are designed for the reduced strength near the weld.

### 8. The method

1. **Need more yield strength?** Pick the mechanism that fits:
   - control the rolling or forging? → **grain refinement**
   - forming the part anyway? → **work hardening**
   - alloy already has solutes? → **solid solution**
   - alloy can grow precipitates and you can hold a furnace schedule? → **precipitation**
2. **Name the obstacle** and **name the price.**
3. If several are active, say which one does most of the work.

**Try one:**

- (a) The same 1045 steel with **10 μm** grains: what's the yield?
- (b) Going from 1% to 4% solute: how much bigger is the strength gain?
- (c) Which mechanism is mainly at work in each: a 6061-T6 bracket, a cold-drawn steel rod, a brass fitting?

You should get (a) about 316 MPa; (b) 2×, since √4 = 2; (c) precipitation, work hardening, solid solution.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 14: Heat treatment is processing, not chemistry

This revisits lesson 9 in more depth. What's new is **how annealing progresses over time**, **the TTT "race"** that decides what steel turns into, and **aging aluminium**. The central message: **chemistry stays the same; only the arrangement changes.** Two parts with identical mill certificates can behave completely differently.

### 1. The blacksmith's chisel

1. Heat to **orange**, plunge in **oil** → hard enough to scratch glass, but **brittle** (dropping it could crack it)
2. Reheat **gently**, far below orange → keeps most of the hardness, stops shattering

Two heats with opposite purposes, on the same steel.

### 2. What the animation shows: annealing in three acts

The graph shows **yield strength vs time** in the furnace, for a cold-worked metal:

| Act | What happens inside | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Recovery** | tangled dislocations rearrange and cancel; internal stress drops | dips **slightly** |
| **2. Recrystallization** | brand-new, defect-free grains form and replace the worked ones | **falls hard**, ductility returns |
| **3. Grain growth** | big grains absorb small ones | drifts **lower** (Hall–Petch in reverse) |

**Most of the softening happens in act 2.** Act 3 is usually unwanted: hold too long or too hot and you lose strength you meant to keep.

**Practical:** a **stress-relief** treatment aims for act 1 only, removing residual stress (say, after welding) without much loss of strength.

### 3. Quenching: the TTT race

Hot steel (austenite, with carbon dissolved) can turn into different structures **depending on how fast it cools**. The TTT diagram is the map of that race:

| Cooling path | Result | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| **Slow**, crosses the curves above the nose | **pearlite** (soft ferrite + carbide layers) | soft, ductile |
| **Hold below the nose** (but above where martensite starts) | **bainite** | strong and fairly tough |
| **Fast**, misses the nose entirely | **martensite** | very hard, very brittle |

**Why martensite is so hard:** carbon gets trapped in an iron lattice it **doesn't fit** in, distorting it into a stretched (tetragonal) shape. That distortion and the resulting microstructure strongly impede dislocation motion.

### 4. Tempering: the worked example

**4140 shaft**, oil-quenched from 850 °C:

| | Hardness | Yield | Elongation | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| As-quenched | ~58 HRC | | very low | a sharp blow can crack it |
| **Tempered 400 °C, 1 h** | ~42 HRC | ~1,300 MPa | **11%** | tough, ready for service |

Tempering lets a **little** carbon move, forms fine carbide particles, and relaxes the distortion just enough. **Untempered martensite rarely survives real service.** Tempered martensite is what gets used.

**Temperature and time are design choices**, picked for the toughness you need.

### 5. Aging aluminium (precipitation hardening, from lesson 13)

Three steps:

1. **Solution treat:** heat to dissolve the copper (or other alloying elements) into the aluminium.
2. **Quench:** trap it, super-saturated.
3. **Age:** hold at a modest temperature while tiny precipitates grow.

**Hardness vs aging time:**

- rises → **peak** (precipitates at the ideal size to block dislocations) → **falls** (overaged: precipitates too big and too far apart)

**Practical examples:**

- **T4** = naturally aged at room temperature; **T6** = solution heat-treated and artificially aged; the specified condition is alloy-dependent
- Some aircraft rivets are kept **in a freezer** after solution treatment to **slow natural aging**, so they stay soft enough to drive. They harden after installation.

### 6. The method

For a part that's too soft, too brittle, or full of residual stress, ask what the structure needs:

| Need | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Soften, clean up | **anneal** past recrystallization |
| Remove stress only | **stress relief** (recovery) |
| Harden steel through | **quench** past the TTT nose, then **temper** |
| Harden aluminium | **solution treat → quench → age** to peak |

**The test:** can you sketch the structure before and after on a napkin (dislocation density, grain size, phase)? If not, you're following a recipe without understanding it.

**Try one:** what went wrong in each case?

- (a) A steel part was quenched but came out soft.
- (b) An aluminium part was aged twice as long as the spec called for and is weaker than expected.
- (c) A cold-worked part was annealed to remove stress, but it lost much more strength than planned.

Answers: (a) it cooled too slowly and crossed the TTT curves above the nose, forming pearlite instead of martensite; (b) it overaged, so the precipitates coarsened past the peak; (c) it went past recovery into recrystallization (and possibly grain growth), because it was held too hot or too long.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 15: Choosing the process

This lesson turns everything from lessons 9–14 into a **decision routine**: given a part's requirements, pick the alloy **and** the processing route, and defend the choice in writing.

### 1. The problem

A bolt needs **800 MPa yield**, **10% elongation**, at **low cost**, made in the **millions**. There are dozens of alloy and treatment combinations. The strongest is brittle, the cheapest is soft, and the toughest is expensive. No single number picks the winner.

### 2. The five-step sequence

1. **Write the requirements as numbers:** strength floor, ductility floor, cost ceiling.
2. **Screen:** throw out anything that misses **any** floor. No partial credit.
3. **Match the mechanism:** what obstacle does each survivor use (lesson 13), and can the shop actually do that process?
4. **Name the process:** temperature, time, deformation.
5. **Verify** against the numbers from step 1.

The sequence exists so you're comparing numbers instead of arguing over favourite materials.

### 3. What the animation shows (the bolt)

Floor: **800 MPa yield, 10% elongation**.

| Candidate | Yield | Elongation | Cost | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold-worked 1045 | 560 | | cheap | ✘ **out**, misses the strength floor |
| Aged 2024 aluminium | 345 | | | ✘ **out**, nowhere close |
| Q&T 1045 | 850 | 12% | 1.9× | ✔ passes, but margin = 850/800 = **1.06** (thin) |
| Q&T 4140 | 1,300 | 11% | 2.3× | ✔ passes, margin = 1,300/800 = **1.6** |

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Floor** | draw the 800 MPa line |
| **2. Screen** | two candidates are out immediately |
| **3. Survivors** | two left |
| **4. Rank** | 4140 costs more but has a much safer margin |

**The screen is a guillotine.** Cold-worked 1045 being cheap doesn't matter. It can't hold 800 MPa, so it's out before ranking starts.

### 4. The decision

**Choose Q&T 4140:** oil quench from 850 °C, temper near 400 °C.

**Why pay more?**

- A margin of **1.06** leaves almost no room for material scatter (lesson 12) or heat-treat variation.
- 4140's alloying gives better **hardenability**: it hardens all the way through a thick bolt section, not just at the surface.

This is broadly why high-grade bolts are made from **quenched-and-tempered medium-carbon alloy steel**.

### 5. No free strength

Every strengthening route costs something (lesson 13):

| Mechanism | Pays with |
|---|---|
| Work hardening | ductility |
| Precipitation | process control, money |
| Quench & temper | heat-treatment cost, distortion risk |
| Grain refinement | the cheapest strength, but needs a controlled rolling schedule |

**Be suspicious of any option that claims to be strong, ductile and cheap all at once.**

### 6. The chain, end to end

For the 4140 bolt:

| Link | Content |
|---|---|
| **Structure** | tempered martensite: trapped carbon, fine carbides |
| **Processing** | oil quench 850 °C + temper 400 °C |
| **Properties** | 1,300 MPa yield, 11% elongation |
| **Performance** | the bolt holds the joint |

**A good design memo names all four links.** If someone could grade it without asking you anything, it's done.

### 7. The method

1. Write the **three numbers**: strength floor, ductility floor, cost ceiling.
2. **Screen out** anything that misses any of them.
3. Among the survivors, pick the **cheapest that clears the floors with real margin**.
4. Name the **process** (temperature, time), the **mechanism**, and **what was traded**.

**Try one:** a bracket needs **≥ 300 MPa yield**, **≥ 15% elongation**, and **≤ 1.5× base cost**.

| Candidate | Yield | Elongation | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annealed 1020 | 350 | 36% | 1.0× |
| Cold-worked 1020 | 700 | 8% | 1.1× |
| 6061-T6 aluminium | 275 | 12% | 2.0× |
| Q&T 1045 | 850 | 12% | 1.9× |

Screen, then pick. Answer: only **annealed 1020** passes all three. Cold-worked 1020 fails ductility, 6061-T6 fails all three, and Q&T 1045 fails ductility and cost. Its margin is 350/300 ≈ 1.17.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 16: Fracture, why cracks run

Until now, "failure" meant the stress got too high everywhere. This lesson covers how a **small crack or sharp corner** can break a part even when the average stress looks safe.

### 1. Stress concentrations: corners and holes multiply stress

Holes, sharp corners, keyways and weld edges make the **local** stress higher than the average:

**σ_local = K_t × σ_nominal**

| Feature | K_t |
|---|---|
| Smooth bar | 1 |
| **Small round hole** | **≈ 3**, so the hole edge sees **3×** the stress |
| Sharp notch | higher |
| **Crack** (zero tip radius) | → **infinite** in theory |

This is pure geometry. It's the same for any material.

**Shop practice:**

- **Round your inside corners** (fillets) instead of leaving them sharp.
- A rounded crack tip reduces an ideal elastic stress concentration, but **stop-drilling is not a general repair approval**. A cracked load-bearing part needs its specified inspection and repair or replacement process.

### 2. Why brittle things break at "weak" stresses (Griffith)

A crack grows when the energy **released** by growing it exceeds the energy **cost** of making new surface.

**Glass example:** its bonds could in theory take gigapascals, but:

| Flaw size | Breaking stress |
|---|---|
| 1 μm | ~211 MPa |
| 1 mm scratch | **~6.7 MPa** |

**A brittle material's strength is set by its biggest flaw, not by its bonds.** That's exactly how **glass cutting** works: score a line, and it snaps cleanly along it.

### 3. Fracture toughness: when does a crack run?

For a real crack, use the **stress intensity factor**:

**K = Y × σ × √(π × a)**

- **σ** = applied stress
- **a** = crack size (here, **half** the total crack length)
- **Y** = geometry factor (1 for a simple central crack)

**It fails when K reaches K_IC**, the material's **fracture toughness**.

### 4. What the animation shows

A steel plate at **200 MPa**, K_IC = **50 MPa√m**, with a **5 mm** (half-length) crack:

| Step | What's calculated | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Toughness** | the material's limit | K_IC = 50 |
| **2. Crack** | K = 200 × √(π × 0.005) | **25.1**, half the limit ✔ |
| **3. Critical** | crack size where K = 50: a_c = (50/200)² ÷ π | **≈ 20 mm** half-length (40 mm total) |
| **4. Double σ** | at 400 MPa: a_c = (50/400)² ÷ π | **≈ 5 mm**, so the existing crack is now critical |

**Critical crack size goes as 1/σ².** Double the stress and the tolerable crack shrinks to **a quarter**. Any time the load changes, the safe crack size has to be recalculated.

**What this doesn't tell you:** whether the crack will **grow** over time from fatigue (next lesson) or corrosion. "Below K_IC" means it won't snap *right now*, not that the crack is safe to leave.

### 5. Toughness depends on temperature

**BCC steels** (lesson 4) have a **ductile-to-brittle transition**. Below a certain temperature, K_IC **collapses**.

**The Schenectady:** a WWII tanker that **cracked in half while tied up at the dock** in cold weather. The steel's toughness dropped and an existing flaw ran.

**"A toughness number without a temperature is a rumour."** For prairie winters, check the toughness at the coldest service temperature.

### 6. Inspection must be able to find it

Work out a_c, then ask: **can our inspection reliably find cracks that size?**

- a_c above the detection limit → a necessary first check, not approval; allow for detection probability, growth, inspection intervals and margin
- **a_c smaller than what you can detect** → **the design has to change** (lower stress, tougher material). Inspection alone won't keep it safe.

### 7. The method

1. **Notches and holes:** multiply the nominal stress by K_t.
2. **Cracks:** K = Yσ√(πa), compared with K_IC (at the service temperature).
3. **Critical size:** a_c = (K_IC ÷ Yσ)² ÷ π.
4. **Compare a_c with your detection limit.**

**Try one:**

- (a) A plate under 100 MPa has a small round hole. What stress does the hole edge see?
- (b) The same steel plate (K_IC = 50) is loaded to **300 MPa**. What's K for the 5 mm crack, and what's the new critical size?

You should get (a) about 300 MPa; (b) K ≈ 37.6 MPa√m (still under 50), with a critical half-length of about 8.8 mm.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 17: Fatigue, death by a thousand cycles

The last lesson covered cracks that snap in one go. This one covers cracks that **grow a little with every load cycle** until the part fails, often at stresses **well below yield**.

### 1. What fatigue is

Bend a paperclip once and it bends. Bend it back and forth 20 times and it **snaps**, even though no single bend was enough to break it.

Repeated loading starts a tiny crack (usually at a corner, scratch or weld edge), then grows it **a bit each cycle**. That's **fatigue**. It's behind most mechanical failures: shafts, axles, springs, bolts under vibration, and trailer frames cracking at weld toes.

### 2. The S-N curve

Plot **stress amplitude (S)** vs **cycles to failure (N)** on log-log axes:

- **Higher stress → fewer cycles** before failure
- On log-log axes it's roughly a **straight line** (Basquin's law)

**σ_a = σ_f′ × (2N)^b**

Rearranged to find life: **N = ½ × (σ_a ÷ σ_f′)^(1/b)**

### 3. What the animation shows

A steel shaft, σ_f′ = 900 MPa, b = −0.1:

| Step | Stress amplitude | Life |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Basquin** | the straight line on log-log axes | |
| **2. 300 MPa** | ½ × (300/900)^(−10) = ½ × 3¹⁰ | **≈ 29,500 cycles** |
| **3. 400 MPa** | ½ × (400/900)^(−10) | **≈ 1,660 cycles** |

**A third more stress cut the life by about 18×.** Fatigue is extremely sensitive to stress level, so small stress reductions buy huge life gains.

**Same number, different clock:** 29,500 cycles is **under an hour** for a shaft vibrating at 10 Hz, but **years** for an aircraft pressurized once per flight. How often it's loaded matters as much as how hard.

### 4. Endurance limit: steel vs aluminium

| Material | Behaviour at low stress |
|---|---|
| **Steel** | curve **flattens** near **½ of ultimate strength**. Some tested steels show an **endurance limit** under specified conditions; corrosion, welds, notches and variable loading can invalidate it. |
| **Aluminium** | **no flat part**. Every cycle does some damage, so it always has a finite life. |

That's why aluminium aircraft are designed for a set life and inspected on a schedule.

### 5. The de Havilland Comet: square windows

The first jet airliner (1950s) had **square-ish windows**. The corners were stress concentrations (lesson 16). Every pressurization cycle grew cracks from those corners, and several aircraft broke up in flight.

**That's why airliner windows have rounded corners.** It's the same reason to round any corner on a part that's loaded repeatedly.

### 6. Paris law: how fast a crack grows

Once a crack exists:

**da/dN = C × (ΔK)^m**, with m ≈ 3 for metals

- **Double ΔK → 8× faster growth** (2³)
- **Example:** at ΔK = 20 MPa√m, growth = 10⁻¹¹ × 20³ = 0.08 μm per cycle. At 40, it's **0.64 μm** per cycle.

**A crack spends most of its life small, then speeds up.** Inspection intervals are set so you'll catch it during the slow part, before the fast part.

### 7. Miner's rule: mixed loads

Real parts see a mix of stress levels. Add up the fraction of life used at each level:

**Damage = Σ (cycles used ÷ cycles available)**. Failure is predicted at **1**.

**Example:**

- 100,000 cycles at a level good for 200,000 → **0.50**
- 100,000 cycles at a level good for 400,000 → **0.25**
- Total = **0.75**, so one quarter of this model's damage budget remains—not necessarily one quarter of the service time

**Caution:** fatigue life **scatters by factors of 3–10**, not by a few percent. Big safety factors on fatigue life are justified.

### 8. Design takeaways

- **Lower the stress range:** small reductions give big life gains.
- **Round corners**, polish surfaces, and grind smooth weld toes. Cracks start at stress raisers.
- Use a fatigue curve for the actual material, surface, environment and loading. An ideal endurance limit is not a maintenance exemption.

### 9. The method

1. **One stress level:** N = ½ (σ_a/σ_f′)^(1/b), or read it off the S-N curve.
2. **Mixed levels:** Miner's sum. Keep it well below 1.
3. **Existing crack:** Paris law. Inspect well before it gets close to critical size (lesson 16).

**Try one:** the same shaft (σ_f′ = 900, b = −0.1):

- (a) What's the life at 250 MPa?
- (b) It runs 10,000 cycles at 300 MPa (life 29,500) and 50,000 cycles at 250 MPa. What fraction of its life is used?

You should get (a) about 183,000 cycles; (b) 0.34 + 0.27 ≈ 0.61, so about 39% of the model's damage budget remains; remaining cycles depend on the future loading.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 18: Creep, failure on a slow clock

Fatigue is failure from repeated loading. Creep is failure from **steady loading plus heat, over time**. A part can **slowly stretch** under a constant load, even below its yield strength, until it fails.

### 1. What creep is

Hang a weight on a hot metal bar and leave it. Day by day, it **keeps stretching**, slowly. At high temperature, atoms move around (diffusion, lesson 8), dislocations climb past obstacles, and grains slide along their boundaries.

**When does it matter?** A common rule of thumb: once a material is above roughly **40% of its melting point in kelvin**.

| Material | Melting point | Creep matters from roughly… |
|---|---|---|
| Steel | ~1,800 K | ~450 °C+ (exhaust, boilers, turbines) |
| Lead | ~600 K | **room temperature** (old lead pipes sag) |
| Many plastics | low | **room temperature** (a loaded plastic shelf bows over months) |

**Where you see it:** turbine blades, exhaust manifold bolts loosening, boiler tubes, furnace parts, and plastic parts under constant load.

### 2. What the animation shows: the three stages

The graph shows **strain vs time** under a constant load and temperature:

| Stage | Shape | What's happening |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Primary** | steep, then slowing | the metal work-hardens a bit |
| **2. Secondary** | **straight line**, steady rate | hardening and softening balance; **most of the life is spent here** |
| **3. Tertiary** | curving up fast | internal voids and necking → **rupture** |
| **4. Design** | | **size the part on the secondary slope** |

**The secondary rate is the design number.** Allowed creep strain divided by a representative steady rate estimates time to that strain limit. It does not by itself predict rupture life.

### 3. Stress and temperature both matter, temperature much more

**Norton's law:** creep rate = A × σⁿ × exp(−Q/RT)

- **Stress exponent n ≈ 3–8:** with n = 5, **20% more stress → 2.5× faster creep** (1.2⁵)
- **Temperature** is in an exponential (like diffusion, lesson 8), so a modest rise can increase the rate by orders of magnitude

**A local hot spot**, not the average temperature, usually decides a hot part's life.

### 4. Larson–Miller: trading time for temperature

Nobody can test for 26 years. So test **hotter and shorter**, then convert:

**P = T × (20 + log₁₀ t)**

- **T in kelvin**, **t in hours**
- The same P at the same stress means the same damage

**The worked example:** a superalloy ruptures after **1,000 h at 800 °C** (1,073 K). What life at **700 °C** (973 K)?

1. P = 1,073 × (20 + log 1,000) = 1,073 × 23 = **24,679**
2. At 973 K: 20 + log t = 24,679 ÷ 973 = 25.36 → log t = 5.36
3. t ≈ **230,000 h ≈ 26 years**

**100 °C cooler → about 230× longer life.** In creep, temperature moves the answer more than stress does.

### 5. Don't over-extrapolate

That 26 years is a **230:1** stretch from the test data. The rule: **extrapolate about one order of magnitude (10×) at most.** Beyond that, the creep mechanism itself may change. Treat 26 years as a hypothesis to confirm with longer tests, not a guaranteed life.

### 6. The grain-size reversal

| | Room temperature | High temperature (creep) |
|---|---|---|
| **Fine grains** | ✔ stronger (Hall–Petch) | ✘ worse, more boundaries to slide along |
| **Coarse grains** | weaker | ✔ better creep resistance |

That's why jet engine turbine blades are often cast as a **single crystal**, with no grain boundaries at all.

### 7. The method

1. **Is it hot enough to creep?** (above ~0.4 × melting point in kelvin)
2. **Design on the secondary creep rate:** allowed strain ÷ rate = approximate time to the strain limit; check rupture separately.
3. **Short hot test → long cooler life** with Larson–Miller (T in K, t in hours).
4. **Don't extrapolate past ~10×** the test time.
5. **Watch for hot spots:** temperature has the biggest effect.

**Try one:**

- (a) Using the same superalloy data (P = 24,679), predict the rupture life at **750 °C** (1,023 K). Is that within the one-decade rule?
- (b) With n = 5, how much faster does it creep if the stress goes up 10%?

You should get (a) log t ≈ 4.12, so t ≈ 13,000 h (about 1.5 years), roughly 13× the test time, so it exceeds this lesson's illustrative 10× caution threshold; (b) 1.1⁵ ≈ 1.6× faster.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 19: Reading a phase diagram

This lesson introduces **the map of an alloy**: for any mix of two metals and any temperature, it tells you **what's present** (liquid, solid, or both) and **what each part is made of**.

### 1. The map

| Axis | Shows |
|---|---|
| **Horizontal** | composition: how much of metal B is mixed in (wt%) |
| **Vertical** | temperature |

Pick a composition and a temperature, find that point on the map, and read which **region** it's in.

**It shows the destination, not the route.** It says what the alloy *wants* to be at equilibrium (if you wait long enough), not how fast it gets there. Speed comes later.

### 2. The regions and lines (copper–nickel)

| Region / line | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **L** (top) | all liquid |
| **α** (bottom) | all solid |
| **L + α** (the lens-shaped middle) | liquid and solid together |
| **Liquidus** (upper line) | first solid appears when cooling past it |
| **Solidus** (lower line) | last liquid disappears |
| **Solvus** (in other diagrams) | the most of B a solid can dissolve before a second phase appears |

### 3. What the animation shows (Cu–30% Ni, cooling slowly)

**Given teaching equations, not a real Cu–Ni phase diagram:** liquidus T = 1085 + 3.7C, solidus T = 1085 + 3.2C (C = % nickel, entered as 30, not 0.30).

| Step | What happens | Temperature |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Cool** | all liquid, dropping down the vertical line at 30% | from 1,400 °C |
| **2. Liquidus** | first solid crystals appear: 1085 + 3.7 × 30 | **1,196 °C** |
| **3. Solidus** | last liquid freezes: 1085 + 3.2 × 30 | **1,181 °C** |
| **4. Tie line** | at 1,190 °C, what are the liquid and solid made of? | see below |

**The 15 °C between liquidus and solidus is the "mushy zone":** part solid, part liquid, like slush. It matters for casting and welding.

**Solder example:** ordinary 60/40 tin-lead solder has a mushy range, so it goes pasty before setting. 63/37 solder melts and freezes at **one** temperature with no mushy zone. (That special composition is covered in later lessons.)

### 4. The tie line: what each phase is actually made of

At 1,190 °C, draw a **horizontal line** across the two-phase region. Where it hits each boundary gives each phase's composition:

- **Liquid** (left end, on the liquidus): (1,190 − 1,085) ÷ 3.7 = **28.4% Ni**
- **Solid** (right end, on the solidus): (1,190 − 1,085) ÷ 3.2 = **32.8% Ni**

**Neither one is 30%.** The alloy is 30% nickel **on average**, but the solid is richer in nickel (the higher-melting metal freezes out first) and the liquid is leaner.

**This is the most common beginner mistake:** confusing the **overall** composition (30%) with the **phase** compositions (28.4% and 32.8%).

### 5. Solubility usually drops as it cools

Every boundary answers the same question: **how much B can this phase hold at this temperature?** Usually the answer **shrinks as it cools**.

**Example:** tin in lead can dissolve **19.2%** at 183 °C, but only **~2%** near room temperature. Cool it and the extra tin has to come out as a second phase.

**That's the basis of precipitation hardening** (lessons 13–14): dissolve hot, cool, and the excess forms the tiny particles that block dislocations.

### 6. The method

1. **Vertical line** at your alloy's composition.
2. **Horizontal line** at your temperature.
3. **Read the region** where they cross: L, α, or L + α.
4. **In a two-phase region:** draw the tie line. Its ends are the compositions of each phase, **not** your alloy's composition.
5. **On a boundary exactly?** Say which side you mean. The line is a transition, not a state.

**Try one:** using the same simplified lines, for a **Cu–40% Ni** alloy:

- (a) At what temperature does it start to freeze?
- (b) At what temperature is it fully solid?
- (c) At 1,220 °C, what are the compositions of the liquid and the solid?

You should get (a) 1,233 °C, (b) 1,213 °C, (c) liquid ≈ 36.5% Ni and solid ≈ 42.2% Ni.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 20: The lever rule

The last lesson found **what** each phase is made of (the tie-line ends). This one finds **how much** of each phase there is. It's a simple mass balance, and the same seesaw idea from the beam reactions lesson.

### 1. Everyday version first

You blend two batches of grain, one at **12%** moisture and one at **18%**, and want **14%** overall. How much of each?

- 14 sits **2** from 12 and **4** from 18 (total gap = 6)
- Fraction of the **18%** batch = 2 ÷ 6 = **⅓**
- Fraction of the **12%** batch = 4 ÷ 6 = **⅔**

You need **more of the batch closer to your target**. That's the whole lever rule.

### 2. The alloy version

In a two-phase region, the alloy's overall composition is a **weighted average** of the two phases:

**C₀ = W_L × C_L + W_α × C_α**, with W_L + W_α = 1

One line of algebra gives:

**W_L = (C_α − C₀) ÷ (C_α − C_L)**   **W_α = 1 − W_L**

### 3. The "opposite arm" rule

Picture the tie line as a **seesaw**, with your alloy (C₀) as the **pivot**:

- **Each phase's fraction = the segment on the *far* side from it ÷ the whole tie line**
- The phase **closer** to your alloy gets **more**: the short arm carries the heavy weight, like the seesaw where the heavier child sits closer

### 4. What the animation shows (Cu–40% Ni at 1,220 °C)

| Step | What's shown | Value |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Tie line** | ends from last lesson | liquid 36.5%, solid 42.2% (length = 5.7) |
| **2. Fulcrum** | the alloy sits at | **40%** |
| **3. Far arm** | for liquid, the arm toward the solid end: 42.2 − 40 | **2.2** |
| **4. Balance** | W_L = 2.2 ÷ 5.7 | **0.39 → 39% liquid, 61% solid** |

**Check:** 40% is closer to the solid end (42.2) than the liquid end (36.5), so **solid should dominate**. 61% ✔

### 5. Built-in checks

- The two fractions **add to 1** ✔
- The phase **nearer** your alloy is the **bigger** one ✔
- Alloy **dead centre** on the tie line → **50/50**
- A fraction **negative or above 1** → your point isn't between the tie-line ends. Re-read the diagram.
- **At the liquidus** → 0% solid. **At the solidus** → 100% solid.

### 6. Mass, not volume

The composition axis is **weight %**, so the lever rule gives **mass fractions**. "61% solid" means 61% of the **mass**, not necessarily 61% of the volume, because liquid and solid have different densities. Convert with densities if a drawing needs volume.

### 7. If you forget which arm goes where

**Don't memorise the picture. Re-derive it** from C₀ = W_L·C_L + W_α·C_α. It takes 30 seconds and can't go wrong.

### 8. The method

1. Draw the **tie line** at your temperature and read its ends (C_left, C_right).
2. **W_left = (C_right − C₀) ÷ (C_right − C_left)**, the far arm over the whole.
3. **W_right = 1 − W_left.**
4. **Check:** they add to 1, and the phase nearer your alloy dominates.

**Try one:**

- (a) Cu–30% Ni at 1,190 °C: from last lesson, liquid = 28.4% Ni and solid = 32.8% Ni. What fraction is liquid, and what fraction is solid?
- (b) Mixing problem: you have bar stock at 0.2% carbon and 0.8% carbon. What fraction of the 0.8% stock gives a 0.4% carbon blend?

You should get (a) W_L = (32.8 − 30) ÷ 4.4 ≈ 0.64, so 64% liquid and 36% solid (it's only just started freezing); (b) (0.4 − 0.2) ÷ 0.6 = ⅓.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 21: Transformations

This lesson puts phase diagrams (lesson 19) and the lever rule (lesson 20) together to trace an alloy **cooling step by step**. It also introduces two important points: the **eutectic**, and the effect of **cooling rate**.

### 1. The eutectic: one special melting point

"Eutectic" comes from Greek for "easily melted." In lead–tin:

**At exactly 61.9% tin and 183 °C:** liquid → α (lead-rich solid) + β (tin-rich solid), **all at once**

- It happens at **one fixed temperature**, like a pure metal freezing, with **no mushy zone**
- That's the **lowest melting point** of any lead–tin mix
- The two solids grow together as **fine alternating layers** (lamellae), because each one pushes the other's atoms aside as it grows

**Practical:** **63/37 solder** is close to eutectic, so it goes from liquid to solid instantly (clean joints). **60/40** is off-eutectic, so it passes through a pasty range first.

### 2. What the animation shows (Pb–40% Sn, cooling from 300 °C)

| Step | Temperature | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Liquidus** | crossing the liquidus | first α crystals start to grow (tree-like "dendrites") |
| **2. Primary α** | cooling toward 183 °C | more α grows; the leftover liquid gets richer in tin |
| **3. Lever rule** | just above 183 °C | W_α = (61.9 − 40) ÷ (61.9 − 19.2) = 21.9 ÷ 42.7 = **51% primary α**; the other **49% is liquid** at exactly 61.9% Sn |
| **4. Eutectic** | at 183 °C | that 49% liquid freezes all at once into fine α + β layers |

**Final structure below 183 °C:**

- **51% primary α**: chunky crystals that formed first
- **49% eutectic**: fine striped α + β mixture

**Only two phases** (α and β), but **two different arrangements**. The "eutectic" is a **microconstituent** (a recognisable structure), not a separate phase.

### 3. Cooling rate changes the story

The diagram is the **destination**. Cooling rate decides whether you get there.

**Coring (microsegregation) in Cu–Ni:**

- The first solid to freeze is nickel-**rich**, and the last is nickel-**poor** (lesson 19's tie lines).
- **Slow cooling:** diffusion evens it out, so everything ends at the average composition.
- **Fast cooling:** no time to diffuse, so each crystal keeps a **nickel-rich core** and a **nickel-poor rim**. You can see it after etching.
- **Fix:** a **homogenizing anneal**. Hold it hot and let diffusion finish the job.

**For the lead–tin casting**, fast cooling gives **less** primary α and a **finer** eutectic than the diagram predicts.

### 4. Iron–carbon: the most important diagram

The same ideas apply, with the highest stakes:

| Feature | Value | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Eutectoid** (like a eutectic, but solid → solids) | **0.77% C, 727 °C** | austenite → **pearlite** (alternating ferrite + cementite layers), the structure of most structural steel |
| **Steel vs cast iron** | **2.14% C** | below = steel, above = cast iron |
| **Quench fast** | skips the diagram | **martensite**: very hard, very brittle (lessons 9 and 14) |

The heat treatments from lessons 9 and 14 are all moves on this map.

### 5. The method

1. **Vertical line** at your composition.
2. **Walk down** in temperature. At each boundary, name what starts to form.
3. **At a eutectic:** use the lever rule **just above** the eutectic temperature to get primary vs eutectic fractions.
4. **Ask the rate question:** slow enough for diffusion to keep up, or fast enough to freeze the hot structure in?
5. **Sketch** the final structure: which phases, how much, and how they're arranged.

**Try one:** using the lead–tin numbers (α = 19.2%, eutectic = 61.9%, β = 97.5% Sn):

- (a) Pb–50% Sn: how much primary α vs eutectic?
- (b) Pb–61.9% Sn: what's the final structure?
- (c) Pb–70% Sn: this is on the tin-rich side, so which phase forms first, and how much?

You should get (a) (61.9 − 50) ÷ 42.7 ≈ 28% primary α and 72% eutectic; (b) 100% eutectic, with no primary crystals; (c) primary β first, (70 − 61.9) ÷ (97.5 − 61.9) ≈ 23% primary β and 77% eutectic.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 22: Four families, four property packs

This lesson zooms out from metals to **all four material families**. Each comes as a **package of properties** set by its bonding (lessons 1–2). The skill is picking the **family** before picking a specific grade.

### 1. The four families

| Family | Bonding | Strengths | Weaknesses | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Metals** | metallic | ductile, conductive, tough | heavy (mostly), corrode | steel, aluminium, copper |
| **Ceramics** | ionic / covalent | hard, heat-resistant, wear-resistant | **brittle** | alumina, SiC, glass, concrete |
| **Polymers** | chains + weak secondary bonds | light, flexible, corrosion-resistant | soft, low temperature limit, creep | polyethylene, nylon, PVC |
| **Composites** | fibres in a matrix | **directional** strength and stiffness, very light | weak across the fibres, matrix temperature limit | carbon fibre, fibreglass |

### 2. Specific stiffness: stiffness per kilogram

When weight matters, compare **E ÷ ρ** (modulus ÷ density), not E alone.

### 3. What the animation shows (a tie rod, stiff in tension, minimum mass)

| Step | Material | E ÷ ρ |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Budget** | stiffness needed, mass to minimise | |
| **2. Metals** | steel: 200 ÷ 7.85 | **25.5** |
| | aluminium: 69 ÷ 2.7 | **25.6** |
| **3. CFRP** | carbon fibre (along the fibres): 140 ÷ 1.55 | **90** |

**The surprise:** **steel and aluminium tie.** Aluminium is a third as stiff but a third as heavy, so for a tension rod there's no weight saving from switching.

**Carbon fibre wins 3.5×**, but **only along the fibres** and only below its resin's temperature limit.

### 4. The right index depends on the shape of the job

| Part | Index to maximise | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Tie rod** (tension) | E ÷ ρ | stiffness scales directly with area |
| **Beam** (bending) | **√E ÷ ρ** | depth helps bending a lot (lesson 20 of physics), so lighter materials can be made thicker |

**Run the beam numbers:**

| | √E ÷ ρ |
|---|---|
| Steel | √200 ÷ 7.85 ≈ **1.8** |
| Aluminium | √69 ÷ 2.7 ≈ **3.1** |
| CFRP | √140 ÷ 1.55 ≈ **7.6** |

For the **geometrically similar beam sections in this comparison**, aluminium scores about **1.7×** higher. Panels with thickness as the free variable use E^⅓/ρ instead. That's why aircraft skins and truck bodies use aluminium, even though it's no better in pure tension.

**Never compare on E alone.**

### 5. Properties come as a bundle

Choosing on **one** property is the classic mistake:

- Want stiffness? It comes with density.
- Want high temperature? Ceramics give it, with brittleness attached.
- Want light weight? Polymers give it, with a low temperature limit.

**"A strength quoted without a temperature and a direction is a rumour."** Always look at the whole package: density, stiffness, strength, temperature limit, and how it fails.

### 6. Selection order

**Family → grade → processing**, never the reverse.

Processing (heat treatment, cold work, layup) moves a material around **within** its family, sometimes by 2–3×. It **never** moves it into another family's territory. No heat treatment makes steel as light as carbon fibre or as heat-proof as a ceramic.

### 7. The method

1. **List the job's non-negotiable demands:** conduct? stay hot? take a hit? light? stiff in one direction?
2. **Ask which demand rules out which family.**
3. **The survivor is your starting family.** Then pick the grade and processing.
4. **State it in one sentence:** the family, the demand that forced it, and what you gave up.
5. **If every family fails**, the requirements need renegotiating.

**Try one:** which family fits each job, and what's the trade-off?

- (a) A furnace lining at 1,400 °C
- (b) A bus bar that carries high current and gets bent to fit
- (c) A lightweight tank for corrosive chemicals at room temperature
- (d) A bicycle frame tube needing maximum stiffness per weight along its length

Answers: (a) ceramic, giving up toughness (it's brittle); (b) metal, probably copper, giving up weight; (c) polymer, giving up temperature limit and stiffness; (d) composite (carbon fibre), giving up cost and strength across the fibres.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 23: Direction and temperature

The last lesson said a property without a direction and temperature is a rumour. This lesson puts numbers on both: **how much weaker the weak direction is**, and **how each material family fails when it gets hot or has a sudden temperature change**.

### 1. Direction: the rule of mixtures

A composite is **stiff fibres** in a **soft matrix** (glue). How stiff it is depends on **which way you pull**.

**Along the fibres (parallel):** fibres and matrix stretch **together**, so the stiff fibres carry most of the load:
**E∥ = V_f × E_f + V_m × E_m** (a simple weighted average)

**Across the fibres (series approximation):** imagine an ideal equal-stress arrangement of fibre and matrix. This gives a useful simple bound, not an exact transverse-modulus model for every composite. The soft matrix stretches most and controls the result:
**1/E⊥ = V_f/E_f + V_m/E_m**

It's the same as **springs in parallel vs in series**: in parallel the stiff one dominates; in series the soft one does.

### 2. What the animation shows (carbon fibre / epoxy)

60% carbon fibre (230 GPa) in 40% epoxy (3 GPa):

| Step | Direction | Calculation | Stiffness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Block** | fibres all one way | | |
| **2. Along** | parallel | 0.6 × 230 + 0.4 × 3 | **139 GPa** (steel-like) |
| **3. Across** | series | 1 ÷ (0.6/230 + 0.4/3) | **7.4 GPa** (like wood) |
| **4. Ratio** | | | **19 : 1** |

Superb along the fibres, weak across them. **Wood, rolled sheet and drawn wire** have the same pattern to a lesser degree (lesson 5's texture).

**Real parts** use layers at different angles (0°, 90°, ±45°) to land between the two extremes.

### 3. Temperature: three families, three ways to fail

| Family | How heat ruins it | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Polymers** | amorphous regions soften near Tg; crystallinity and crosslinks also matter | use the grade's stiffness and creep data at service temperature; not all polymers operate below Tg |
| **Metals** | under sustained load above **~0.4 × melting point (K)**, **creep** (lesson 18) | a screening flag, not a sharp onset temperature; 0.4 × 933 K for Al is about 100 °C |
| **Ceramics** | handle steady heat fine, but a **sudden** temperature change cracks them (**thermal shock**) | E × α × ΔT vs strength |

### 4. Thermal shock, worked

An alumina part sees a **sudden 500 K drop**:

σ ≈ E × α × ΔT = 300,000 MPa × 8×10⁻⁶ × 500 = **1,200 MPa**

Its tensile strength is only ~300 MPa, so this fully restrained estimate is **4× the supplied tensile strength**. It flags a risk; actual transient thermal stress depends on geometry, gradients, cooling rate and constraints. (This is the thermal stress formula from physics lesson 27. The cold surface tries to shrink while the hot interior holds it back.)

**Everyday example:** a hot glass dish plunged into cold water can crack. **Borosilicate** glass (Pyrex-type) has about **⅓ the thermal expansion** of ordinary glass, so this simplified restrained-strain comparison gives about ⅓ the stress. It is not a guarantee against thermal shock or a cookware-use instruction.

### 5. Design to the worst case

| Selection (comparing candidates) | Design (making it survive) |
|---|---|
| best-edge values, to be fair | **worst-case** values |

- Composite: design with the **across-fibre** stiffness unless the load direction is guaranteed.
- Polymer: check service-temperature stiffness, creep, Tg and any melting/degradation limits for that grade.
- Metal under steady load: stay **below creep onset**.

**If only the brochure direction or brochure temperature works, you don't have a design.**

### 6. The method

1. **Which direction is it loaded?** Use the parallel or series formula for that direction.
2. **What's the service temperature?** Check each family's failure mode:
   - polymer → Tg
   - metal under load → 0.4 Tm (K)
   - ceramic → thermal shock (EαΔT vs strength)
3. **Both the weak direction and the hot limit** must clear the demand with margin.

**Try one:**

- (a) **Fibreglass:** 50% glass fibre (E = 72 GPa) in 50% epoxy (3 GPa). What's the stiffness along and across, and the ratio?
- (b) **Thermal shock:** compare ordinary glass (E = 70 GPa, α = 9×10⁻⁶/K) with borosilicate (E = 63 GPa, α = 3.3×10⁻⁶/K) for a sudden 100 K drop.

You should get (a) about 37.5 GPa along and 5.8 GPa across, a ratio of about 6.5 : 1; (b) about 63 MPa for ordinary glass vs about 21 MPa for borosilicate, roughly ⅓ the stress.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 24: Choosing under constraints

Lesson 15 used screen-then-rank to choose a **process**. This lesson does the same one level up, to choose a **material family**. It adds one rule: **always say what the winner costs you.**

### 1. Screens vs trade-offs

| | Screen | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | a must-have; violating it = failure | a preference among survivors |
| **Examples** | service temperature, corrosive environment with no coating allowed, a hard weight limit | cost, machinability, supplier, familiarity |
| **Used for** | **eliminating** | **ranking** |

**Order matters: screen first, rank second.** Rank first and the cheap, familiar but wrong material can win.

**Judge each family at its best**, its champion grade. If even that can't clear the bar, the elimination is fair.

### 2. What the animation shows (bracket at 900 °C)

Requirement: hold **50 MPa at 900 °C continuously**, in air, bolted to a steel frame.

| Step | Family | Limit | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Screen** | draw the 900 °C line | | |
| **2. Kill** | polymers | ~250 °C | ✘ |
| | composites | matrix ~250 °C | ✘ |
| | ordinary steel, aluminium | creep from ~450 °C | ✘ |
| **3. Candidates for detailed checks** | nickel superalloy | check creep life, grade and geometry | ✔ but ~**20× steel cost** |
| | ceramic (alumina, SiC) | check brittle strength, creep, mounts and thermal cycling | ✔ |
| **4. Winner** | **ceramic**, on cost and oxidation resistance | | **price: brittle** |

### 3. Saying the price out loud

The decision isn't finished at the winner's name. Finish it with **what you gave up and how the design compensates**:

> **Ceramic is the classroom shortlist choice for the 900 °C requirement, pending grade-specific checks. Price: brittleness, plus a thermal expansion mismatch with the steel frame.** Compensate with **flexible (compliant) mounts** and **generous corner radii**.

**Why the mismatch matters:** steel expands about 12×10⁻⁶/K and alumina about 8×10⁻⁶/K. Heated 900 °C, bolted rigidly together, the difference creates thermal stress (physics lesson 27), and the brittle ceramic is the one that cracks. Flexible mounts let them grow by different amounts.

**What each family costs you:**

| Family | Gives you | Costs you |
|---|---|---|
| Ceramic | high temperature | brittleness |
| Composite | stiffness per weight | directionality, low matrix temperature |
| Polymer | cheap, corrosion-proof | soft, creeps |
| Metal | ductile, familiar | weight |

### 4. Process and environment are screens too

- **Manufacturing:** ceramics don't take threads well; composites don't suit rush orders. If you can't make the shape, at the volume, on time, the family is out.
- **Environment:** UV breaks down many polymers; salt water pits unprotected steel; temperature cycling loosens joints.

**The O-ring:** the Space Shuttle Challenger's O-rings had a known cold-temperature limit. It was written down and **overridden** on a cold launch morning. **Write your constraints down, and make relaxing a screen a signed, named decision.**

### 5. The method

1. **List every constraint:** temperature, load, environment, shape, volume, cost.
2. **Mark each one** as a screen or a trade-off.
3. **Apply the screens** at each family's best edge.
4. **Rank the survivors** on the trade-offs.
5. **State the winner + the requirement that forced it + its price + the compensation.**
6. **No survivors?** A constraint has to move, and someone signs off on that.

**Try one:** an outdoor hopper liner must handle −40 °C to +60 °C, UV, salt spray (no coating allowed), and impacts **without shattering**, at **low cost**. Candidates: plain carbon steel, stainless 304 (~4× cost), UV-stabilised polyethylene, glass-ceramic, carbon fibre.

Screen, rank, and state the price. Answer: carbon steel fails corrosion (no coating) and glass-ceramic fails impact. Stainless, UV-stabilised PE and carbon fibre survive. Ranked on cost, **UV-stabilised PE** wins. Its price is low stiffness and creep, so support it with ribs or a backing frame.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 25: Screen, then rank

This lesson formalises the last few. **Screens** remove what can't work. Then an **index**, derived from what the part actually has to do, ranks what's left. It also shows where those indices come from, so you aren't stuck when the loading changes.

### 1. The Ashby chart

Plot **stiffness (E)** vs **density (ρ)** on log-log axes. Each family forms a **bubble**:

- **Metals:** upper right (stiff, heavy)
- **Polymers:** lower left (floppy, light)
- **Composites:** stretched along the top (stiff and light)
- **Ceramics:** stiff, medium density

A line of **constant E/ρ** runs **diagonally** (slope 1) across the chart. Everything above the line beats everything below it.

### 2. What the animation shows (a 1 m tie rod)

Requirements: a set stiffness, **≤ $10/kg**, outdoor, **no coating**.

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Plot** | steel, aluminium and carbon fibre on the chart |
| **2. Index** | E/ρ: steel **25.5**, aluminium **25.6**, carbon fibre **90.3** |
| **3. Screens** | carbon fibre: **$80/kg** ✘ cost; bare steel: **rusts** ✘ corrosion |
| **4. Shortlist** | **6061 aluminium** |

**The index alone would pick carbon fibre**, then put aluminium and steel in a tie. **The screens decide it.** That's why you screen first.

### 3. Where the index comes from (tie rod example)

You don't have to memorise indices. Derive them in three lines:

1. **Objective:** minimise mass, m = ρ × A × L
2. **Constraint:** fixed stiffness, k = E × A ÷ L → so A = kL ÷ E
3. **Substitute:** m = ρ × (kL ÷ E) × L = **k L² × (ρ ÷ E)**

To minimise mass, **maximise E/ρ**. The length and stiffness are fixed by the job, so only the material term is left.

### 4. Different jobs, different indices

| Part | Goal | Index to maximise |
|---|---|---|
| Tie rod | stiff, light | **E / ρ** |
| Tie rod | strong, light | **σ / ρ** |
| **Beam** | stiff, light | **E^½ / ρ** |
| Beam | strong, light | σ^⅔ / ρ |
| **Panel / plate** | stiff, light | **E^⅓ / ρ** |

**Why the exponents change:** in bending, thickness helps a lot (depth cubed, physics lesson 20), so a lighter material can be made **thicker** to make up for being less stiff. The more bending-dominated the job, the more low density wins.

**Given comparison data:** use E in GPa and ρ in g/cm³ consistently. Wood: E ≈ 10 GPa along grain, ρ ≈ 0.6 g/cm³. These index values carry compound units and are only compared on the same basis.

| | Tie (E/ρ) | Beam (E^½/ρ) | Panel (E^⅓/ρ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steel | 25.5 | 1.8 | 0.75 |
| Aluminium | 25.6 | 3.1 | 1.5 |
| Carbon fibre | **90** | **7.6** | 3.4 |
| **Wood** | 17 | 5.3 | **3.6** |

Wood **loses** as a tie, **beats aluminium** as a beam, and **beats everything** as a stiff panel. That's why floors, joists and old aircraft used wood. **The right index depends on the loading.**

### 5. Relaxing a screen is a decision

Want to keep steel in the running for outdoors? That's allowed, but only as a **named decision with a named cost**: a coating budget, a maintenance schedule, a heavier section. It shouldn't be a quiet fudge to keep a favourite candidate alive.

### 6. The trade study

When 2–3 survivors are close, decide on what the index **ignored**: cost, corrosion, fatigue, availability, embodied energy. Then **write it down, naming the rejects and why**. That document is the trade study.

### 7. The method

1. **List the non-negotiables** and delete every candidate that fails any one.
2. **Derive the index:** write mass in terms of the free size variable, eliminate it using the constraint, and read off the material group.
3. **Rank the survivors** by that index.
4. **Close race?** Decide on the ignored columns. **Write it up** with the rejects named.

**Try one:** you need a **stiff, light floor panel** for an indoor trailer (no corrosion issue, cost ≤ $10/kg). Candidates: steel, aluminium, carbon fibre ($80/kg), wood. Screen, then rank with E^⅓/ρ.

Answer: carbon fibre fails the cost screen. Ranked on E^⅓/ρ: **wood (3.6)** > aluminium (1.5) > steel (0.75). Wood wins, and that's why trailer decks are often wood. Its price is moisture sensitivity and lower durability.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 26: Corrosion is a design load

So far, failure came from loads, heat and time. This lesson adds the **environment**. Corrosion isn't a maintenance detail to deal with later. Treat it as a load you design against from the start.

### 1. Corrosion is a battery

For metal to corrode, you need a complete electrical **cell**:

| Part | Role | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Anode** | the metal that **dissolves** | aluminium cleat |
| **Cathode** | the metal that's **protected** | steel bolt |
| **Electrolyte** | the liquid that carries ions between them | salt spray, road salt slush, condensation |
| **Metal contact** | carries electrons | the bolt touching the cleat |

**Interrupt the cell and you can reduce or stop that galvanic pathway.** Other corrosion mechanisms may still operate on the same part. Every protection method works by breaking one of them.

### 2. Which metal dissolves? The galvanic series

Metals ranked by electrical potential in seawater. **The more negative one is the anode and dissolves:**

| More negative (dissolves) → | → More positive (protected) |
|---|---|
| zinc → **aluminium (−0.75 V)** → **carbon steel (−0.60 V)** → stainless (passive) → copper | |

**The bigger the voltage gap, the stronger the drive.**

**Useful side of this:** **galvanized steel** is coated with zinc, which is more negative than steel, so the zinc dissolves **instead of** the steel, even where it's scratched. Boats bolt on **zinc anodes** for the same reason.

### 3. Area ratio: how fast

The voltage gap sets the **drive**. The **area ratio** sets the **speed**:

| Setup | Result |
|---|---|
| **Small** cathode, **large** anode | attack spread thin → slow ✔ |
| **Large** cathode, **small** anode | attack concentrated → **fast, local failure** ✘ |

### 4. What the animation shows (steel bolt in an aluminium cleat, salt spray)

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Joint** | steel bolt through aluminium, wetted by salt spray |
| **2. Anode** | aluminium is 0.15 V more negative, so **aluminium dissolves** |
| **3. Current** | electrons flow from aluminium to steel |
| **4. Crevice** | under the bolt head, pits start |

**The area ratio helps here:** a small bolt on a big cleat spreads the attack. **Flip it** (aluminium rivets in a steel plate) and the rivets go fast.

**The real threat is the crevice** under the bolt head (next section).

### 5. Crevices and pitting: no second metal needed

- **Crevice corrosion:** in a tight gap (under a washer, gasket or bolt head), oxygen gets used up. The starved metal inside becomes the anode. That's why rust **hides under washers and in lap joints**.
- **Pitting:** chloride (salt) punches small holes in the protective film on aluminium and stainless, and they don't heal. The surface looks fine while deep pits grow underneath.

**The joint you designed for strength is also where corrosion starts.**

### 6. Stress-corrosion cracking (SCC)

It needs **all three at once**:

1. **Tensile stress** (applied or left over from welding or forming)
2. **A specific environment** (like chlorides for stainless steel)
3. **A susceptible alloy**

**Remove any one and it stops.** Fixes: lower the stress (stress-relief anneal, shot peening), change the alloy, or keep the environment away.

**Signature:** fine **branching cracks** with little visible metal loss. By the time you see them, it's already failed.

### 7. Protection = break the cell

| Method | What it breaks |
|---|---|
| Paint, sealant, coating | keeps the **electrolyte** away |
| **Nylon washer and sleeve** | breaks the **metal contact** |
| Zinc anode, galvanizing | gives a **sacrificial anode** |
| Drain holes, no crevices | stops the **electrolyte** collecting |
| Stress relief | removes the **SCC stress** |

**For the boat cleat:** a stronger bolt **won't help**. Isolate it (nylon washer and sleeve), bed it in sealant, and set inspection intervals from the actual exposure, component rating and consequences—not a universal seasonal interval. **The cheapest fix is the one designed in before the parts exist.**

### 8. The method

1. **Find the cell:** anode (more negative), electrolyte, metal contact.
2. **Find the amplifier:** small anode? crevice? tensile stress?
3. **Match what you see to the mechanism:** pits, attack at joints, branching cracks.
4. **Break the cell**, and name the inspection for anything you couldn't protect.

**Try one:**

- (a) Copper water pipe joined directly to galvanized steel pipe. Which corrodes, and what's the usual fix?
- (b) Why does a scratched galvanized fence post still not rust at the scratch?
- (c) Aluminium rivets hold a steel panel on a truck exposed to road salt. Good idea?

Answers: (a) the galvanized side (zinc and steel) corrodes; plumbers fit a dielectric union to break the metal contact; (b) the zinc around the scratch is more negative and dissolves to protect the exposed steel; (c) bad idea, because small aluminium anodes against a large steel cathode corrode fast. Isolate and seal, or change the fastener.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 27: Embodied impacts and repair

This lesson adds **energy and emissions** to the selection table, next to stiffness, strength and cost. The key idea: **it isn't a material property on its own. It's the material times how the part is used over its life.**

### 1. Embodied energy

Making a material takes energy, measured in **MJ per kg**. Multiply by the part's mass:

**impact = mass × embodied intensity**

The page's teaching values:

| Material | MJ/kg |
|---|---|
| Primary aluminium (from ore) | **200** |
| Steel | **30** |
| **Recycled aluminium** | **10** (about 5% of primary) |

That **20:1 gap** between primary and recycled aluminium is bigger than almost any property difference between competing materials, so it can change decisions.

### 2. What the animation shows

A **2 kg aluminium** bracket vs a **3 kg steel** bracket for the same job:

| Step | Comparison | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Gate** (just made) | primary Al: 2 × 200 = **400 MJ**; steel: 3 × 30 = **90 MJ** | aluminium **4× worse** |
| **2. Recycled** | recycled Al: 2 × 10 = **20 MJ** | **beats steel** before it's even used |
| **3. Drive** (in a vehicle, 200,000 km) | steel's extra 1 kg burns ~1.5 MJ per 1,000 km → **300 MJ** of extra fuel | |
| **4. Break-even** | steel: 90 + 300 ≈ **390** vs primary Al: **400** | about even |

**The same bracket on a stationary rack?** No fuel savings, so **steel wins easily** (90 vs 400).

**"Sustainability is not a material property. It is a material property times a life."** The use phase has to be calculated, not assumed.

### 3. Recyclability depends on the whole system

| Material | Recycling reality |
|---|---|
| **Thermoplastics** (PE, PP, nylon) | can be remelted ✔ |
| **Thermosets and most composites** (fibreglass, carbon fibre) | cannot simply be remelted like thermoplastics; mechanical, thermal or chemical recovery routes depend on the resin, fibres and available facilities |
| **Steel** | very recyclable, but copper contamination in scrap limits high-grade reuse |
| **Aluminium** | very recyclable if alloys are kept separate |

"Recyclable" on paper only counts if the product can actually be **taken apart and collected**.

### 4. Repair: the longest life is the one already built

Every extra year in service **spreads the embodied energy** over more use. Design for repair:

| Repair-friendly | Repair-hostile |
|---|---|
| **bolted** joints | welds, structural glue |
| **replaceable wear parts** (bolt-on wear plates, bushings) | wear surfaces built into the main part |
| accessible joints, documented disassembly | hidden fasteners, sealed assemblies |

A bolt-on cutting edge or wear plate means replacing a small part instead of the whole implement.

### 5. The method

1. **Add MJ/kg (and CO₂/kg)** to the candidate table.
2. **Multiply by part mass** → embodied impact.
3. **Compare with the use phase:** does a lighter part save fuel over its life? How much, over how long?
4. **Ask the repair questions:** can it come apart? Are wear parts separable? Does the joint outlast the product?
5. **Name the end of life:** recycle, remanufacture, or landfill, and the design feature that makes it possible.

**Try one:** using the same brackets (primary Al 400 MJ, steel 90 MJ, 1 kg difference, 1.5 MJ per kg per 1,000 km):

- (a) The vehicle only drives **50,000 km** in its life. Which bracket has lower total energy?
- (b) At what distance do primary aluminium and steel break even exactly?
- (c) Which option has the lowest energy in the two stated classroom scenarios?

You should get (a) steel: 90 + 75 = 165 MJ vs 400 MJ; (b) about 207,000 km, since 310 MJ ÷ 1.5 MJ per 1,000 km; (c) recycled aluminium, at 20 MJ, which wins whether it moves or not.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 28: The synthesis method for materials

This is the materials version of physics lesson 28. There's no new science. It's the habit of **tracking where every number came from**, so you can work out what went wrong when a prediction misses.

### 1. What the animation shows (a carburizing miss)

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Predict** | 950 °C for 4 h → 2√(Dt) = **0.80 mm** (lesson 8) |
| **2. Measure** | actual depth: **0.55 mm**, about 30% short |
| **3. Ledger** | "furnace at 950 °C" was marked **assumed**, not checked |
| **4. Actual** | the thermocouple read **60 °C high**; the furnace was really at **~890 °C** |

**Check it:** at 890 °C (1,163 K), D drops to about 5.2×10⁻¹² m²/s, and 2√(Dt) ≈ **0.55 mm** ✔. That agrees at the reported precision; it supports checking the temperature but does not uniquely prove the cause.

**The square-root maths was right. The assumption was wrong.** Diffusion depends exponentially on temperature (Arrhenius), so a small temperature error becomes a big depth error.

### 2. Provenance: every number comes from somewhere

Every property number traces back to one of three sources:

| Source | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | bonding, crystal, defects | "FCC, so ductile when cold" |
| **Processing** | what was done to it | "quenched and tempered at 400 °C" |
| **Test** | measured on this part or batch | "hardness checked at 42 HRC" |

Also mark each one as **assumed, derived, or measured**.

**"A number with no source is a rumour wearing units."** If you catch yourself writing "steel is strong," ask: **which** steel, **which** treatment, **which** test?

### 3. The ledger (three columns, like physics lesson 28)

| Claim | What breaks if it's false | What would expose it |
|---|---|---|
| Furnace held 950 °C (assumed) | diffusion depth way off | check with a second thermocouple |
| 6061-T6 yields at 275 MPa (datasheet) | if it was welded, the area near the weld is overaged and weaker (lesson 13) | hardness test near the weld |
| Service stays above −20 °C | BCC steel goes brittle (lessons 4 and 16) | toughness test at the coldest temperature |

### 4. Check each handoff

A real materials argument chains the whole course:

**bonding → family → processing → curve → toughness → selection**

**Say each link in a sentence.** For example: *"The quench trapped zinc and magnesium in solution, so after aging the 7075 yields at 505 MPa."* If you can't say the connection in words, there's a gap.

### 5. Limit checks

Push each variable to an extreme. Does the answer make sense?

| Formula | Limit | Should give | Sensible? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beam droop δ = FL³/3EI | E → ∞ (infinitely stiff) | δ → 0 | ✔ |
| Critical crack a_c = (K_IC/σ)²/π | σ → 0 (no load) | a_c → ∞ in the ideal zero-applied-stress model; not a real crack-safety verdict | ✔ |
| Hall–Petch σy = σ₀ + k/√d | d → ∞ (one giant grain) | σy → σ₀ in the fitted equation; extrapolation to a single crystal requires caution | ✔ |

**Check limits inside the model's valid domain.** A failure there may expose an error; behaviour outside the domain may instead show that the approximation has ended.

### 6. The method

1. **Open the ledger first:** for each claim, note its source (structure, processing, test), what breaks, and what would show it.
2. **Chain it:** structure → processing → properties → performance, with each link in a sentence and units checked.
3. **Test the limits.**
4. **When prediction and measurement disagree, check the ledger before the arithmetic.**
5. **Name the assumption most likely to be wrong**, and the test that would prove it.

**Try one:** you predicted a heat-treated 4140 part at 42 HRC, but it measured 50 HRC (harder and more brittle). Write two ledger lines for likely causes, and say what test would confirm each.

Possible answers: "tempered at 400 °C (assumed)" — the furnace may have run cold, giving less tempering; check with a calibrated thermocouple. "Temper time 1 h (assumed)" — it may have been pulled early; check the furnace log. Either one leaves more untempered martensite, which is harder and more brittle.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 29: The spar, end to end

This is the materials capstone. One small wing spar is run through the whole course, and **strength turns out not to be the deciding factor**.

### The setup

- 100 g glider, with a gust giving **2.5× its weight** in lift
- Each half-wing is **250 mm** long, with a spar **4 mm wide × 6 mm deep** (6 mm is the bending depth), fixed at the root
- Requirement: tip bends **no more than 5 mm**
- Candidates: **balsa**, **7075-T6 aluminium**, **carbon fibre**

### Link 1: Load (physics, Newton)

Total gust lift = 0.1 kg × 9.81 × 2.5 = **2.45 N**. Each half-wing carries half: 1.23 N, spread evenly.

### Link 2: Root bending moment (physics, torque)

The spread load acts as if concentrated at the middle of the half-span:
M = 1.23 N × 0.125 m = **0.153 N·m**

### Link 3: Stress (physics, bending)

- I = b × h³ ÷ 12 = 4 × 6³ ÷ 12 = **72 mm⁴**
- c = half the depth = 3 mm
- σ = M × c ÷ I ≈ **6.4 MPa**, the same for all three materials, since it's the same shape and load

### Link 4: Strength check (materials, allowables)

| Material | Allowable (strength ÷ FoS) | Margin vs 6.4 MPa |
|---|---|---|
| Balsa | 35 ÷ 2 = 17.5 MPa | **2.7×** |
| 7075-T6 | 505 ÷ 1.5 = 337 MPa | **53×** |
| Carbon fibre | 1,200 ÷ 1.5 = 800 MPa | **125×** |

**Everything passes on strength, easily.** Nothing is close to failing.

### Link 5: Stiffness check (physics, beam deflection)

Tip droop = w × s⁴ ÷ (8 × E × I), where w = load per metre:

| Material | E | Droop | vs 5 mm limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balsa | 3 GPa | **11.1 mm** | ✘ **too floppy** |
| 7075-T6 | 71.7 GPa | 0.46 mm | ✔ |
| Carbon fibre | 135 GPa | 0.25 mm | ✔ |

### Link 6: Mass (materials, selection)

Spar volume = 4 × 6 × 250 mm³ = 6 cm³:

| Material | Mass |
|---|---|
| Balsa | 0.96 g (lightest, but failed stiffness) |
| 7075-T6 | 16.9 g |
| **Carbon fibre** | **9.6 g** |

### What the animation shows

| Step | Check | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Strength** | all far from yielding | no one eliminated |
| **2. Stiffness** | 5 mm limit | **balsa out** |
| **3. Mass** | aluminium vs carbon | **carbon wins** |

### The call

**Carbon fibre.** **Stiffness** eliminated balsa, and **mass** eliminated aluminium. Strength was checked and passed; it did not decide between the surviving options.

### The main point

**Calculate every margin and let the smallest one tell you what drives the design.** If you'd assumed "it's a strength problem," you'd have optimised the wrong thing. For light, slender parts, **stiffness usually governs**, which is why the stiffness indices (E/ρ, √E/ρ) from lesson 25 exist.

**Change the requirement and the answer changes.** That's why the decision has to record *why*.

### Sanity checks the page mentions

- **Fatigue:** the stress is far below long-life fatigue strength (lesson 17).
- **Fracture:** a predicted critical crack larger than the section warns that the ideal large-plate crack model is outside its geometric range. Check finite-section failure, defects and the material-specific mode; this is not an inspection approval.

### Every link cites its lesson

Load (Newton) → moment (torque) → stress and deflection (bending) → allowables (lesson 12) → fatigue and fracture (lessons 16–17) → T6 processing (lessons 13–14) → ranking (lesson 25). **A chain you can audit link by link is a decision you can defend.**

**Try one:**

- (a) Keep balsa but make the spar **8 mm deep** (4 × 8). Recompute I, the droop, and the mass. Does it pass now, and how does it compare with carbon?
- (b) If the deflection limit tightened to **0.3 mm**, which original candidates survive?

You should get (a) I ≈ 171 mm⁴, droop ≈ 11.1 × 72/171 ≈ 4.7 mm ✔ passes, and mass ≈ 1.3 g, **much lighter than carbon's 9.6 g**. Going deeper beats switching materials, which is the beam index in action. (b) Only carbon fibre (0.25 mm); aluminium's 0.46 mm now fails.

## Materials 101 · Lesson 30: The closed-book mastery check

Like the physics check, this explains **how the end-of-course test works**. It also includes a **failure-diagnosis cheat sheet** and a self-review list.

### 1. The format

- **12 questions, one sitting, closed book**
- **4 chain** (carrying a result from one topic into another)
- **4 failure** (diagnosing how something broke)
- **4 mixed** (bonding, phase diagrams, selection, properties)
- **Pass: 9 of 12 (75%)**, which clears the 70% bar

### 2. The two-part gate

1. **Score ≥ 70%**, **and**
2. **A written correction for every missed chain or failure question**

What the animation shows:

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Sit** | 12 questions in three rows |
| **2. Score** | 9 ✓, 3 ✗ → 75% |
| **3. Repairs** | corrections filed for the missed chain and failure items (the missed mixed item needs none) |
| **4. Gate** | score + repairs → **opens** to Engineering 101 |

Below 70%: retake, **and still file the corrections**, because the repair is where the learning happens.

### 3. A sample correction (the lever rule)

**Missed:** answered **67% β** instead of **33%**.

1. **Error:** "I used the near arm. A phase's fraction comes from the **opposite** segment."
2. **Re-derivation:** tie line from 20 to 80, alloy at 40 → W_β = (40 − 20) ÷ (80 − 20) = **⅓**
3. **Failed instinct:** "I read the diagram like a **ruler** instead of a **balance**."

Three sentences, and the mistake becomes something you understand rather than a forgotten guess.

### 4. Failure questions: diagnose first, calculate second

Name the failure mode **from the evidence**, then let the numbers confirm it:

| Evidence | Mode | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Steady load + heat + long time, slow stretching | **creep** | 18 |
| Repeated loading, beach marks, started at a corner or weld | **fatigue** | 17 |
| Sudden snap, flat sparkly surface, cold or notched | **brittle fracture** | 16 |
| Branching cracks, little metal loss, tensile stress + chlorides | **stress-corrosion cracking** | 26 |
| Dissimilar metals in salt water, the more negative one eaten | **galvanic corrosion** | 26 |
| Hidden rust under washers or in lap joints | **crevice corrosion** | 26 |
| Necked, dull, fibrous cup-and-cone | **ductile overload** | 6 and 11 |

"Numbers without a mode are just arithmetic."

### 5. Chain questions: the usual crossings

Can you carry a number across each of these without notes?

- **Grain size → strength** (Hall–Petch)
- **Temperature → D → case depth** (Arrhenius → 2√(Dt))
- **Phase diagram → tie line → fractions** (lever rule, **opposite** arm)
- **Load → moment → stress and deflection → margin** (the spar)
- **Test data → mean − 2s → ÷ FoS → allowable**
- **Crack size + stress → K → compare with K_IC**
- **Stress amplitude → cycles (Basquin) → Miner sum**

### 6. Quick self-review

- Bonding → property package (conducts? bends? melting point?)
- FCC / BCC / HCP → ductility; **BCC goes brittle in the cold**
- Anneal / quench / temper / age: **which defect move**, and which property changes
- **Stiffness ≠ strength ≠ hardness ≠ toughness**
- **Screen first, then rank**, with the **right index** for the loading (E/ρ, √E/ρ, ∛E/ρ)
- **State the winner's price**
- **Corrosion cell:** anode, cathode, electrolyte, contact. Break one.
