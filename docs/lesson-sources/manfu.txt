## Manufacturing · Lesson 1: The act

This starts a new course, **Manufacturing**. Engineering lesson 16 screened processes by tolerance and volume. This lesson goes one step earlier: **what physically has to happen to the material?**

### 1. Five physical acts

Every process makes shape by doing **one** of these:

| Act | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Freeze** | pour liquid, let it solidify | casting, injection moulding |
| **Deform** | push a solid into a new shape | bending, forging, rolling, stamping |
| **Cut** | remove chips | milling, turning, drilling, sawing, grinding |
| **Join** | connect separate pieces | welding, bolting, gluing |
| **Add** | build up layer by layer | 3D printing |

**A mill, a lathe and a saw are the same act (cut) with different tooling.** Name the act first, then the machine.

### 2. Let geometry rule acts out

Before cost even comes up, the **shape** eliminates options:

| Geometry | Rules out | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A void no tool can reach | **cut** | cutting needs a path **in and out** |
| An undercut | a simple **rigid die** (freeze, deform) | the part can't be pulled out of the die |
| A one-off part | a **mould** (as a first choice) | too much tooling cost for one part |

### 3. What the animation shows

| Step | What's shown |
|---|---|
| **1. Acts** | the five acts: freeze, deform, cut, join, add |
| **2. Pocket** | an **open pocket**: a drill or mill has a path in and out → **cut** works |
| **3. Tunnel** | a **closed tunnel** inside a lattice: no rigid tool can get in → **no path** |
| **4. Verdict** | pocket → **cut**; tunnel → **add** |

### 4. The worked example

- **500 identical brackets** with a pocket a drill can reach → **cut** (or **deform**), since the tool has a path in and out
- **One lattice** with an internal tunnel → **add** (3D printing), since no rigid tool can reach it

**The machine brand hasn't come up yet.** The geometry decided first.

### 5. The act also changes the material

What you specified is really **the strength of a process history**, not just a name on a datasheet (Materials lessons 9 and 13–14):

- **Casting** freezes grains in place (and may leave porosity)
- **Bending** leaves residual stress and springback
- **Welding** changes the metal next to it (the heat-affected zone)

**Specify the part, then ask what the act does to it.**

### 6. The method

1. **Name the act:** freeze, deform, cut, join, or add.
2. **Let geometry rule acts out:** tool access, undercuts, one-off vs volume.
3. **Then** pick the machine.
4. **Check what the act does** to the material's properties.

**Try one:** which act fits each?

- (a) A hollow shaft with internal cooling channels that twist inside the wall
- (b) 10,000 identical steel L-brackets from flat sheet
- (c) A hitch receiver made of a square tube plus a mounting plate
- (d) A one-off replacement gear for an old machine

Answers: (a) **add**, since no tool can reach twisting internal channels; (b) **deform**: punch and bend the sheet, high volume, simple shape; (c) **join**: weld the tube to the plate; (d) **cut**: machine it from bar stock, since a mould isn't worth it for one part.## Manufacturing · Lesson 2: The chip

This lesson is about the **cut** act from lesson 1: what sets the **force** on the tool, and what sets the **power** the machine needs. They depend on different knobs.

### 1. How metal cutting actually works

The tool doesn't **split** metal the way an axe splits wood. It pushes the metal ahead of the edge up through a thin **shear zone**, and it peels off as a **chip**. Real details (tool angle, friction) matter, but a simple model gets the scaling right.

### 2. Force follows the chip's cross-section

**F = u × b × h**

- **b** = width of cut (mm)
- **h** = uncut chip thickness (mm), how thick a layer each pass takes
- **u** = specific cutting energy (N/mm²), how hard the material is to cut

**Thicker or wider chip → more area sheared → more force.** A tougher material (higher u) means more force for the same chip.

### 3. Power is force × speed

**P = F × v** (cutting speed)

**Speed doesn't change the force in this model. It changes the power** (and the heat).

### 4. What the animation shows (steel cut)

b = 3 mm, h = 0.10 mm, u = 2,500 N/mm²

| Step | What's shown |
|---|---|
| **1. Layer** | the 0.10 mm layer ahead of the tool |
| **2. Shear** | it shears up into a chip |
| **3. Force** | F = 2,500 × 3 × 0.10 = **750 N** |
| **4. Knobs** | **2× thickness → 1,500 N** · **2× speed → same 750 N, but 2× power** |

### 5. Putting numbers on power

At a cutting speed of **100 m/min** (≈ 1.67 m/s):

- P = 750 N × 1.67 m/s ≈ **1.25 kW**
- Double the speed → **2.5 kW**, with the same force on the tool

**Another way to see it:** u is also **energy per volume of metal removed** (N/mm² = J/mm³). So **power = u × removal rate**. Here: 3 × 0.10 mm × 1,667 mm/s = 500 mm³/s, and 2,500 × 500 = 1.25 kW ✔.

### 6. Why it matters

- A **heavy, slow** cut and a **light, fast** cut can draw the same power but load the tool very differently.
- **Force** matters for tool breakage, deflection and chatter, and for holding the part.
- **Power** matters for the motor and for heat at the edge.
- "Faster spindle" doesn't automatically mean "harder cut." **Say which knob you turned.**

**Note from the page:** tool sharpness isn't in the formula. It's a model of the **chip**, not of a fresh edge. A dull tool raises the force beyond this estimate.

### 7. The method

1. **Force = u × width × chip thickness.**
2. **Power = force × cutting speed.**
3. **Changing the chip** changes force (and power). **Changing the speed** changes only power and heat.

**Try one:**

- (a) Same steel, but double the **width** to 6 mm (h stays 0.10). What's the force?
- (b) Your lathe has **2 kW** available for cutting. For the original 750 N cut, what's the fastest cutting speed?
- (c) Aluminium is much easier to cut. Assume **u ≈ 700 N/mm²**. What's the force for the original chip (3 × 0.10 mm)?

You should get (a) **1,500 N**; (b) 2,000 ÷ 750 ≈ 2.7 m/s, about **160 m/min**; (c) 700 × 0.3 = **210 N**.## Manufacturing · Lesson 3: Springback

This lesson is about the **deform** act: why a bend **opens up** after you let go, and which materials open more.

### 1. Why it happens

When you bend sheet metal, part of the bend is **plastic** (it stays) and part is **elastic** (it springs back). The stress-strain curve from Materials lesson 10 is at work here. When you release the punch, the elastic part recovers and **the bend opens**.

**If you want 90°, you have to bend past 90°**, or use a die that forces the bend to set.

### 2. Which materials spring back more

The key ratio is **yield strength ÷ stiffness (σy ÷ E)**:

**high yield + low stiffness → more elastic strain stored → more springback**

| Material | σy / E | Springback |
|---|---|---|
| Mild steel (250 MPa / 200 GPa) | 0.0013 | **low** |
| Aluminium (270 MPa / 70 GPa) | 0.0039 | medium |
| Titanium (880 MPa / 110 GPa) | **0.0080** | **high** |

**Thin sheet** and a **large bend radius** also mean more springback, because the elastic part is a bigger share of the total bend.

### 3. The quick formula (sharp bends)

**Curvature lost ≈ 3 × σy ÷ (E × t)**

(curvature = 1 ÷ radius)

The final angle follows because the **arc length stays the same**: final angle ≈ bent angle × (R ÷ R_final).

### 4. What the animation shows

A **1 mm** strip bent **90°** over a **15 mm** radius:

| Step | Aluminium | Titanium |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Bend** | 90°, R 15 mm | 90°, R 15 mm |
| **2. Release** | radius grows to **18.1 mm** | radius grows to **23.2 mm** |
| **3. Compare** | opens **15.6°** (ends ≈ 74°) | opens **31.8°** (ends ≈ 58°) |

**Same punch, same bend, but titanium opens twice as much.** Don't set the die to the angle on the drawing.

### 5. Too gentle a bend: it springs flat

If the radius is so large (or the sheet so thin and strong) that the metal **never yields**, the bend is entirely elastic and **springs back flat**:

**springs flat if 1/R ≤ 2σy ÷ (E × t)**

For 1 mm aluminium: 2 × 270 ÷ 70,000 = 0.0077/mm, so any radius **over about 130 mm** won't hold a bend at all.

That isn't a failure to push hard enough. The metal never had to stay bent.

### 6. Shop practice

- **Overbend** on the press brake to allow for springback.
- **Bottoming or coining:** press hard enough into the die that the bend sets more fully.
- **Switching material?** Predict which way the springback moves **before** making parts. Swapping mild steel for a high-strength steel or aluminium will need re-adjustment.

### 7. The method

1. **Compare σy ÷ E.** The higher it is, the more it opens.
2. **Estimate:** curvature lost ≈ 3σy ÷ (Et).
3. **Final angle** ≈ bent angle × R ÷ R_final.
4. **Check** it actually yielded at all (1/R > 2σy/(Et)).
5. **Set the die past the target angle.**

**Try one:**

- (a) 1 mm **mild steel** (250 MPa, 200 GPa), 90° on a 15 mm radius. How much does it open?
- (b) **2 mm** aluminium, same bend. Does it open more or less than the 1 mm sheet, and roughly how much?

You should get (a) curvature lost ≈ 0.00375/mm → R_final ≈ 15.9 mm → final ≈ 85°, so it opens **about 5°**; (b) **less**: curvature lost halves to ≈ 0.0058/mm → R_final ≈ 16.4 mm, so it opens **about 8°** instead of about 16°.## Manufacturing · Lesson 4: Freeze last

This lesson is about the **freeze** act (casting). Metal **shrinks as it solidifies**, so the question is **where the shrinkage hole ends up**: in the part you keep, or in a lump you cut off.

### 1. How a casting freezes

Molten metal freezes **from the mould walls inward**. Heat escapes through the surface, so:

- **Thin sections** (lots of surface for their volume) freeze **early**
- **Chunky sections** (little surface for their volume) stay liquid **late**

**The last place to freeze is where the shrinkage cavity forms.** Metal shrinks as it solidifies, and the last liquid pocket has nothing left to feed it.

### 2. Chvorinov's rule

**Freeze time = C × (V/A)²**

- **V/A** = volume ÷ surface area (the **casting modulus**), a measure of "chunkiness"
- **C** = a mould and metal constant, which **cancels** when comparing shapes in the same mould
- **Squared:** a little more chunkiness means a lot longer to freeze

### 3. The riser: a sacrificial reservoir

A **riser** is an extra lump of metal attached to the casting. Its job is to **stay liquid longest**, keep feeding molten metal into the shrinking part, and **take the shrinkage hole itself**. Then you cut it off.

**Rule: the riser's V/A must be bigger than the section it feeds.** A knob that freezes first is decoration, not a riser.

### 4. What the animation shows

A **120 × 80 × 20 mm** plate, fed by a cylindrical riser (height = diameter).

**Plate:** V = 192,000 mm³, A = 27,200 mm² → **V/A = 7.06 mm**
**Riser** (H = D): **V/A = D ÷ 6**

| Step | Riser | Riser V/A | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Pour** | | | |
| **2. Thin riser** | D = 30 mm | 5 mm | **freezes first** → **void left in the plate** ✘ |
| **3. Plates** | plate | 7.06 mm | |
| **4. Last liquid** | D = 60 mm | 10 mm | freezes **(10 ÷ 7.06)² ≈ 2× later** ✔ shrinkage stays in the riser |

**Minimum riser:** D ÷ 6 > 7.06 → **D > 42 mm**. Real practice adds margin and checks feeding paths (the page notes that's a simplified estimate).

### 5. Directional solidification

The goal: freezing should progress **from the far end of the part toward the riser**, so there's always liquid metal behind the freezing front to feed it. **Never feed a thick section through a thin one.** The thin neck freezes first and cuts off the supply.

**Watch for "hot spots":** a thick boss on a thin plate freezes last and gets shrinkage porosity unless it has its own riser.

### 6. Bonus: freezing speed sets grain size

The same V/A number does a second job:

- **Thin section → freezes fast → fine grains → stronger** (Hall–Petch, Materials lesson 5)
- **Thick section → slow → coarse grains → weaker**

A thick and a thin section **in the same casting won't have the same strength.**

### 7. The method

1. **V/A of the section** being fed.
2. **Riser V/A must be bigger.** For H = D cylinders, V/A = D/6.
3. **Freeze-time ratio** = (V/A ratio)².
4. **Check directional freezing:** thick sections fed by thicker risers, never through thin necks.

**Try one:** a **200 × 100 × 30 mm** plate.

- (a) What's its V/A?
- (b) What's the minimum riser diameter (H = D)?
- (c) With an 80 mm riser, how much later does the riser freeze?

You should get (a) V = 600,000, A = 58,000, so V/A ≈ **10.3 mm**; (b) D > 6 × 10.3 ≈ **62 mm**; (c) riser V/A = 13.3, so (13.3 ÷ 10.3)² ≈ **1.7× later**.## Manufacturing · Lesson 5: Beside the weld

This lesson is about the **join** act (welding). It ties back to Materials lessons 13–14 and Engineering 14: **the weakest part of a weld is usually not the weld metal. It's the strip of plate right beside it.**

### 1. A weld has three zones

| Zone | What happened | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Weld metal (filler)** | melted and re-froze, a tiny casting (lesson 4) | usually **stronger** than the plate |
| **Heat-affected zone (HAZ)** | **never melted**, but got hot enough to change | often the **weak line** |
| **Base metal** | stayed cool | original properties |

### 2. What the animation shows

| Step | What's shown |
|---|---|
| **1. Weld** | the filler bead between two plates is stronger than the plate |
| **2. HAZ** | the band beside the bead where heat changed the metal |
| **3. Strength** | strength across the joint dips in the HAZ, which is the **weak line** |
| **4. More heat** | more heat per length → **wider** weak band |

### 3. What the heat does to the HAZ (depends on the material)

| Material | HAZ effect | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **6061-T6 aluminium** | precipitates **overage** → much weaker | Materials 13 |
| **Cold-worked metal** | **annealed** → softer | Materials 14 |
| **Mild steel** | **grain growth** → coarser, less tough | Materials 5 |
| **Hardenable steel** (4140) | can form **martensite** on cooling → hard and **brittle**, prone to cracking | Materials 9 |

That last one is why hardenable steels often need **preheat** and controlled cooling.

### 4. More heat = wider weak band

**HAZ width grows with √(heat input per length).**

Welders calculate heat input as:

**heat input (kJ/mm) ≈ volts × amps × 60 ÷ (1,000 × travel speed in mm/min)**, times an arc efficiency factor

Slower travel or higher amps → more heat per mm → wider HAZ.

### 5. Two opposite failures

| Failure | Cause | Fixed by a stronger filler? |
|---|---|---|
| **Lack of fusion** | **too little heat**: the filler never actually tied the plates together | ✘ no |
| **Cooked HAZ** | **too much heat**: a wide band of softened or coarsened plate | ✘ no |

**Buying a stronger filler doesn't fix either one.** And **turning the heat up doesn't make the joint stronger.**

### 6. The method

1. **Judge the joint, not the filler:** weld metal, fusion line, HAZ, base metal.
2. **Ask what heat does to this material:** overage, anneal, grain growth, or harden?
3. **Control heat input:** enough for full fusion, not so much that the HAZ is wide and weak.
4. **Design with the HAZ strength** (Engineering lesson 14's ~70% for 6061-T6).

**Try one:**

- (a) A weld at **24 V, 200 A**, travelling **300 mm/min**, arc efficiency 0.8. What's the heat input?
- (b) Double the travel speed. What happens to heat input and HAZ width?
- (c) A welded 6061-T6 bracket broke right beside the bead, not in it. Why, and would a stronger filler have helped?

You should get (a) 24 × 200 × 60 ÷ (1,000 × 300) × 0.8 ≈ **0.77 kJ/mm**; (b) heat input **halves**, and HAZ width shrinks to about 1/√2 ≈ **71%** (but check you still get full fusion); (c) the HAZ was **overaged** and weakened, and a stronger filler **wouldn't help**, because the filler wasn't the weak link.## Manufacturing · Lesson 6: The spread

Engineering lesson 17 set **tolerances** on the drawing. This lesson checks whether the **process** can actually hit them. There are two separate questions: **how wide is the spread**, and **where is it centred**?

### 1. The window and the pile

- **The drawing** gives a **window**: 10.00 ± 0.10 mm, so 9.90 (LSL) to 10.10 (USL).
- **The process** makes a **pile** of parts with a **mean** and a **spread** (standard deviation σ, from Engineering lesson 20).

A perfect nominal on the drawing doesn't put the pile in the middle. A tight window doesn't shrink the pile. **Both have to be true at once.**

*(Here σ means statistical spread, not stress.)*

### 2. Cp: does the pile fit, if centred?

**Cp = window width ÷ 6σ**

Cp **ignores where the pile sits**. It only asks if it *could* fit.

### 3. Cpk: does it actually fit, where it is?

**Cpk = distance from the mean to the nearer limit ÷ 3σ**

Shift the mean and **Cp stays the same, but Cpk drops**.

A common shop gate is **Cpk ≥ 1.33** (the nearer limit is 4σ away). That's a **policy**, not a law of nature, so name it as one.

### 4. What the animation shows (pins)

Window 9.90–10.10 mm, σ = 0.025 mm (6σ = 0.15, 3σ = 0.075).

| Step | Mean | Cp | Cpk | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Centred** | 10.00 | 0.20 ÷ 0.15 = **1.33** | 0.10 ÷ 0.075 = **1.33** | ✔ |
| **2. Shift +0.06** | 10.06 | **1.33** (same width) | | |
| **3. Cpk** | | | 0.04 ÷ 0.075 = **0.53** | ✘ a tail of pins goes over 10.10 |

**Same spread, same Cp, but the pile walked toward one wall.**

### 5. What those Cpk numbers mean in scrap (for a normal distribution)

| Cpk | Nearer limit is… | Out of spec on that side |
|---|---|---|
| **1.33** | 4σ away | about **30 per million** |
| **0.53** | 1.6σ away | about **5–6%** |

That's a big jump in scrap from a 0.06 mm drift.

### 6. Fixing it: two different problems

| Problem | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Off-centre** | Cp fine, Cpk low | **re-centre**: tool offset, adjust the setup (usually cheap) |
| **Too wide** | Cp low | **reduce variation**: better fixturing, machine, tooling, material (usually costly) |

**Always check Cpk.** Quoting a good Cp as proof the parts are in spec is like saying the truck fits the garage while it's parked half on the lawn.

The page notes this assumes a **stable, roughly normal** process. A drawing tolerance doesn't tell you the process σ. You have to measure it.

### 7. The method

1. **Measure** the process mean and σ.
2. **Cp** = (USL − LSL) ÷ 6σ, the width check.
3. **Cpk** = nearer-limit distance ÷ 3σ, the location check.
4. **Compare with the gate** (e.g. 1.33, as a stated policy).
5. **Low Cpk with good Cp → re-centre. Low Cp → reduce spread.**

**Try one:** a bushing bore spec is **25.00 ± 0.08 mm**. The process runs at mean **25.03 mm**, σ = **0.02 mm**.

- (a) What are Cp and Cpk?
- (b) Does it pass a 1.33 gate?
- (c) What's the cheapest fix, and what would Cpk become?

You should get (a) Cp = 0.16 ÷ 0.12 = **1.33**, Cpk = (25.08 − 25.03) ÷ 0.06 = **0.83**; (b) **no**; (c) **re-centre** to 25.00 with a tool offset, and Cpk becomes **1.33**.## Manufacturing · Lesson 7: The stack

This revisits tolerance stacks (Engineering lesson 17) with one new emphasis: **name the bet you're making**, and connect it to last lesson's centring.

### 1. Two ways to add tolerances

| Method | Formula (n equal tolerances) | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Worst case** | **n × tolerance** | a **promise**: every assembly fits, even the unlucky one |
| **RSS** | **tolerance × √n** | a **bet**: independent, centred errors rarely all point the same way |

**Why √n?** If errors are random and independent, some go long and some go short, and they partly cancel. For 3 parts, √3 ≈ 1.73 instead of 3.

**RSS is smaller because you gave up the promise.**

### 2. What the animation shows

Three blocks, each **±0.20 mm**, must fit a **±0.50 mm** allowance.

| Step | Method | Stack | vs ±0.50 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Allowance** | | | ±0.50 |
| **2. Worst case** | 3 × 0.20 | **±0.60** | ✘ doesn't fit |
| **3. RSS** | 0.20 × √3 | **±0.35** | ✔ fits |

### 3. Say which bet you're making

- **Worst case misses, RSS fits** → you're **betting**, not discovering. Say so in writing (and let consequence decide, as in Engineering 17).
- **Both miss** → tighten a part or open the allowance.
- **Never average** 0.60 and 0.35 into a "pass." That isn't a method.

### 4. The link to last lesson: RSS needs centred processes

RSS assumes each part's variation is **random and centred**. If a process has **drifted** (low Cpk, lesson 6), every part leans the same way, and **the shifts add straight up**, not as √n.

**Example:** if each of the three blocks runs **0.05 mm long** on average:

- shifts add directly: 3 × 0.05 = **0.15**
- plus the random spread: ±0.35
- total ≈ **0.50**, right at the limit, even though RSS alone said 0.35

**"A stack is the spread lesson with neighbours."** Check each part's centring before trusting RSS.

### 5. The method

1. **Worst case = sum** of tolerances.
2. **RSS = √(sum of squares)**, or tolerance × √n for equal ones.
3. **Compare both** with the allowance.
4. **State the bet:** promise (worst case) or statistics (RSS).
5. **If using RSS, confirm** the processes are centred (good Cpk) and independent.

**Try one:**

- (a) **Four** parts, each **±0.15 mm**, into a **±0.40 mm** allowance. Worst case? RSS?
- (b) What tolerance per part would pass **worst case**?
- (c) If all four come from one machine that runs **0.04 mm long**, what's the realistic stack using RSS for the spread?

You should get (a) worst case **±0.60** ✘, RSS 0.15 × 2 = **±0.30** ✔; (b) **±0.10** each; (c) 4 × 0.04 = 0.16 shift + 0.30 spread ≈ **0.46**, which **fails** the 0.40 allowance. The shared drift broke the RSS bet.

## Manufacturing 201 · Lesson 1: The force in the roll gap

This starts **Manufacturing 201**. The first lesson is about **rolling** (a deform process): how much force the rolls need to squeeze a strip thinner.

### 1. The dough analogy

Roll dough thinner and take a bigger bite: the rolling pin touches the dough over a **longer patch**. **The force follows that patch length**, not the thickness removed in straight proportion.

### 2. Contact length

**L ≈ √(R × Δh)**

- **R** = roll radius
- **Δh** = draft (thickness removed in one pass)

It grows with the **square root** of the draft. Take 4× the bite and the contact length only doubles.

### 3. Rolling force

**F ≈ flow stress × width × L**

**Flow stress** is the stress needed to keep the metal deforming plastically, roughly its yield strength at the rolling temperature and strain.

### 4. What the animation shows

Stock is **10 mm** thick.

| Step | Exit | Draft Δh | Force |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. 8 mm exit** | 8 mm | 2 mm | **520 kN** |
| **2. 5 mm exit** | 5 mm | 5 mm | **820 kN** |
| **3. Square root** | | 2.5× the draft | only √2.5 ≈ **1.58×** the force (520 × 1.58 ≈ 820) |

The dashed "in proportion" line would have predicted 1,300 kN. Reality follows the square root.

### 5. Friction adds a "hill"

This simple model **leaves out friction**. In reality, friction between the rolls and the strip makes pressure build up toward the middle of the contact (the **friction hill**), so **real roll force is higher**. The √(RΔh) contact length is still the starting point.

### 6. Why it matters

- **Bigger rolls = longer contact = more force** for the same draft. That's why mills rolling thin, hard strip use **small work rolls** supported by larger **backup rolls**: small rolls keep the contact short and the force down, and the backups stop them bending.
- **Planning passes:** a heavy draft costs force, but not in direct proportion, which helps decide how many passes a mill needs.

### 7. The method

1. **Contact length** L = √(R Δh).
2. **Force** ≈ flow stress × width × L.
3. **Remember:** force scales with **√draft**, not draft.
4. **Add margin** for friction, which this model leaves out.

**Try one:** roll radius **200 mm**, strip width **300 mm**, flow stress **150 MPa**.

- (a) For a **3 mm** draft, what are the contact length and the force?
- (b) If you increase the draft to **12 mm** (4×), what happens to the force?

You should get (a) L = √(200 × 3) ≈ **24.5 mm**, F ≈ 150 × 300 × 24.5 ≈ **1.1 MN**; (b) L doubles, so the force **doubles to about 2.2 MN**, not 4×.## Manufacturing 201 · Lesson 2: Speed spends the tool

Manufacturing lesson 2 showed that cutting faster raises **power and heat**, not force. This lesson shows the cost of that heat: **tool life drops sharply when you speed up.**

### 1. The whittling analogy

Whittle slowly and the knife stays sharp. Race, and the edge is gone **long before** you've whittled proportionally more.

### 2. Taylor's tool-life equation

**V × Tⁿ = C**

- **V** = cutting speed (m/min)
- **T** = tool life (minutes until "worn")
- **n, C** = constants for a particular tool, workpiece and wear definition

Rearranged: **T = (C ÷ V)^(1/n)**

With **n = 0.2**, that's **T = (C ÷ V)⁵**, so speed is raised to the **fifth power**. Small speed changes cause big life changes.

### 3. What the animation shows (n = 0.2, C = 200)

| Step | Speed | Tool life |
|---|---|---|
| **1. 100 m/min** | 100 | (200/100)⁵ = **32 min** |
| **2. 150 m/min** | 150 (+50%) | (200/150)⁵ ≈ **4.2 min** |
| **3. Constant** | | V × T^0.2 stays **200** throughout |

**50% faster cost about 87% of the tool life**, roughly an eighth of the life left.

### 4. Price what you spent

Faster cutting means each part takes less time, but **fewer parts per edge**:

**Example:** a part needs 200 m of cutting path.

| Speed | Cut time per part | Tool life | Parts per edge |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 m/min | 2.0 min | 32 min | **16** |
| 150 m/min | 1.33 min | 4.2 min | **about 3** |

So there's a **best speed** that balances machine time against tool cost and tool-change time. Running flat out isn't automatically cheapest.

### 5. Say what "worn" means

C and n only mean something with a **definition of failure**, often a set **flank-wear land** width (how far the worn strip on the tool's side has grown). Change the wear criterion and the constants change. **Quoting C and n without it is meaningless.**

### 6. The method

1. **T = (C ÷ V)^(1/n)** for your tool, material and wear criterion.
2. **Before speeding up**, calculate the life you'll lose (it falls much faster than speed rises).
3. **Price it:** parts per edge, tool cost, change time vs machine time saved.
4. **State the wear criterion** with the constants.

**Try one:** using C = 200, n = 0.2:

- (a) What's the tool life at **120 m/min**?
- (b) What speed gives a **60-minute** tool life?

Back to casting (Manufacturing lesson 4). This time it's about **size**: metal shrinks as it cools, so the **mould has to start bigger** than the drawing.

### 1. The ice-tray analogy

Ice ends up smaller than the compartment you filled. If you needed the ice to match a drawing, **the compartment had to start bigger**.

### 2. Shrinkage allowance

The **pattern** is the shape you use to make the mould cavity.

**pattern = part size × (1 + shrink fraction)**

### 3. What the animation shows (aluminium, 1.3% shrink)

| Step | What's shown | Size |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Pattern** | the cavity is made oversize: 200 × 1.013 | **202.6 mm** |
| **2. Cool** | the poured metal fills it, then shrinks 1.3% | |
| **3. Drawing** | the cold casting matches the drawing | **200 mm** ✔ |

**Never cut the pattern to the cold drawing dimension.** You'd get a short casting.

### 4. The percentage depends on the metal (and the mould)

1.3% is a teaching value for aluminium. Different metals shrink differently. Typical textbook ballparks are roughly 1% for grey iron and about 2% for steel, but **look up the value for the alloy you're pouring**. Restraint from cores and the mould shape also change it. **Don't reuse 1.3% because it was on this page.**

### 5. Machining allowance: a second addition

Shrink gets you to the **as-cast** size. If a face will be **machined** (a mounting face, a bearing bore), the casting needs **extra metal on that face** to cut away.

**Order:** add the machining stock to the part dimension first, then scale the whole thing for shrink. The cold casting has to include that stock.

Only add machining stock to faces you'll actually cut.

### 6. The method

1. **Start from the drawing size.**
2. **Add machining stock** to faces that will be machined.
3. **Multiply by (1 + shrink)** for the alloy you're pouring.
4. **That's the pattern size.**

**Try one:**

You should get (a) 150 × 1.010 = **151.5 mm**; (b) (300 + 3) × 1.02 ≈ **309.1 mm**.## Manufacturing 201 · Lesson 4: Why the weld bows

Manufacturing lesson 5 covered what welding does to **strength** (the HAZ). This one covers what it does to **shape**: why welded plates bow, and which lever controls it most.

### 1. Why it happens

The weld bead and the hot band around it **want to shrink as they cool**, but they're stuck to cold plate that **doesn't**. The compromise is a **bow**, and a thin sheet "potato-chips." It's the same idea as constrained thermal expansion (physics lesson 27) and the welding distortion mentioned in Materials lesson 3.

### 2. The scaling (a teaching model)

**bow ∝ heat input ÷ thickness²**

| Lever | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Double the heat** | 2× the bow |
| **Double the thickness** | **¼ the bow** |

**Why thickness is squared:** a thicker plate has a stiffer section resisting the bend (bending stiffness grows fast with thickness, physics lesson 20).

### 3. What the animation shows

| Step | Heat | Thickness | Bow |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Base** | 100 J/mm | 6 mm | **3 mm** |
| **2. Double heat** | 200 J/mm | 6 mm | **6 mm** |
| **3. Double thickness** | 100 J/mm | 12 mm | **0.75 mm** |
| **4. Powers** | | | heat once, thickness squared |

### 4. The coefficient is made up; the powers are the lesson

The constant was chosen so 100 J/mm on 6 mm gives 3 mm. **Don't quote "3 mm" for a real weld.** Quote the **scaling**: heat to the first power, thickness squared.

The page also notes that **weld sequence and clamping can matter as much as either.**

### 5. Common shop ways to control distortion

These are standard practice (beyond the page's model):

- **Don't over-weld:** a bigger bead than needed is extra heat.
- **Balance welds:** alternate sides so the pulls cancel.
- **Intermittent or skip welding:** spread the heat out.
- **Back-step welding:** short segments laid against the direction of travel.
- **Clamp or fixture** while it cools.
- **Pre-set:** bend it slightly the opposite way so it pulls back straight.

### 6. The method

1. **More heat → proportionally more bow.**
2. **More thickness → bow falls with t².**
3. For flatness, **thickness is the big lever**. A small heat tweak does much less.
4. **Use sequence, balance and clamping** for the rest.

**Try one:** using the teaching scaling (base: 100 J/mm, 6 mm → 3 mm bow):

- (a) 6 mm plate at **150 J/mm**?
- (b) **10 mm** plate at 100 J/mm?
- (c) What thickness would **halve** the original bow at the same heat?

You should get (a) **4.5 mm**; (b) 3 × (6/10)² ≈ **1.1 mm**; (c) t = 6 × √2 ≈ **8.5 mm**.## Manufacturing 201 · Lesson 5: Six motions, then stop

This lesson is about **fixturing**: how to hold a part so it can't move while you cut it, using exactly enough contact points and no more. It's known as the **3-2-1 locating principle**.

### 1. A free part has six ways to move

| Slides | Turns |
|---|---|
| left–right | spin flat (about vertical) |
| front–back | tip forward–back |
| up–down | tip side to side |

**Locating** means removing all six.

### 2. The 3-2-1 rule

| Face | Contacts | Removes |
|---|---|---|
| **Base** (primary) | **3** | up–down + both tips (3 motions) |
| **Side** (secondary) | **2** | one slide + spin (2 motions) |
| **End** (tertiary) | **1** | the last slide (1 motion) |
| **Total** | **6** | **all 6** ✔ |

**motions left = 6 − min(base, 3) − min(side, 2) − min(end, 1)**

**Why three on the base?** Three points define a plane. A **three-legged stool never wobbles**, but a four-legged chair on an uneven floor rocks on whichever leg is high.

### 3. What the animation shows (a block in a milling vise, seen from above)

| Step | Contacts | Motions left |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Base** | 3 under | 3 left |
| **2. Side, end** | + 2 on the side + 1 on the end | **0** ✔ |
| **3. Fourth dot** | + a 4th on the base | **still 0**, and it removes nothing |

**There is no seventh motion to take.** The extra contact just **argues** with the other three, and the part **rocks on the high one** (or gets distorted when clamped). That's over-constraint.

### 4. The other mistake: too few

**2 on the base + 2 on the side + 1 on the end = 5**, leaving **1 motion**: the part can still tip. "Almost located" isn't located.

### 5. Locators vs clamps (general shop practice)

- **Locators** (the 3-2-1 points) decide **where** the part sits.
- **Clamps** push the part **against** the locators to hold it there. They shouldn't fight the locators or define the position themselves.

Aim clamps at or near the locators so clamping force doesn't bend the part.

### 6. The method

1. **Base:** up to 3 contacts.
2. **Side:** up to 2.
3. **End:** 1.
4. **Count:** motions left should be **zero**.
5. **Point out any wasted contacts** (more than 3, 2 or 1 on a face). They add rocking, not location.

**Try one:** how many motions are left, and are any contacts wasted?

- (a) 3 on the base, 1 on the side, 1 on the end
- (b) 4 on the base, 2 on the side, 1 on the end
- (c) 3 on the base, 2 on the side, 0 on the end

You should get (a) **1 left**: the part can still spin flat, because the side needs 2; (b) **0 left**, with **one wasted** base contact (rocking risk); (c) **1 left**: it can still slide along the side.

## Manufacturing 301 · Lesson 1: A larger hole may sit farther off

This starts **Manufacturing 301**, opening with a GD&T idea (geometric dimensioning and tolerancing): **bonus tolerance**. It explains why a hole that's **bigger** is allowed to be **further off-centre**, but only when the drawing says so.

### 1. The peg-and-hole idea

A loose peg in a big hole can sit off-centre and still go in. A peg in a barely-big-enough hole has to be almost perfectly centred. **Extra size = extra room for position error.**

### 2. Maximum material condition (MMC)

**MMC is the size with the most material, which means the tightest fit:**

| Feature | MMC |
|---|---|
| **Hole** | its **smallest** allowed size |
| **Pin / shaft** | its **largest** allowed size |

### 3. Position tolerance is a diameter

A position callout like **⌀0.20** means the hole's **centre (axis)** must sit inside a **circle 0.20 mm across**, centred on the true position. That's a **radial** offset of at most **0.10 mm**, not 0.20.

### 4. Bonus tolerance

If the position is specified **at MMC** (the circled-M symbol on the drawing), then:

**allowed zone ⌀ = stated tolerance + (actual size − MMC size)**

The **bonus** is how much bigger than MMC the hole actually came out.

### 5. What the animation shows

Hole MMC = **10.00 mm**, position **⌀0.20 at MMC**.

| Step | Hole size | Bonus | Zone ⌀ | Max radial offset |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. MMC** | 10.00 | 0 | 0.20 | 0.10 mm |
| **2. Bonus** | grows with size | | | |
| **3. 10.2 mm** | 10.20 | **0.20** | **0.40** | **0.20 mm** |

The drawing's 0.20 didn't change. The extra came from the **clearance** the bigger hole provides.

### 6. Only if the drawing grants it

- **At MMC** (Ⓜ symbol) → bonus allowed.
- **RFS** (regardless of feature size, the default in current ASME practice when there's no modifier) → **no bonus**. The stated zone applies whatever the size.

**Read the callout before spending the bonus.**

### 7. Measuring the position error

From measured x and y offsets of the hole centre:

**position ⌀ = 2 × √(Δx² + Δy²)**

Compare that with the allowed zone diameter.

### 8. The method

1. **Find MMC:** smallest hole or largest pin.
2. **Check the callout:** is it at MMC (Ⓜ)?
3. **Bonus = |actual size − MMC|.**
4. **Allowed zone ⌀ = stated + bonus.**
5. **Measured position ⌀ = 2√(Δx² + Δy²).** Pass if it's within the allowed zone.

**Try one:** a hole is toleranced **8.00–8.30 mm** with position **⌀0.10 at MMC**. It measures **8.15 mm**, and its centre is off by **Δx = 0.06, Δy = 0.07 mm**.

- (a) What's the bonus and the allowed zone?
- (b) What's the measured position ⌀? Pass or fail?
- (c) Would it pass if the callout were RFS (no bonus)?

You should get (a) bonus 0.15, zone ⌀ **0.25**; (b) 2 × √(0.0036 + 0.0049) ≈ **⌀0.18**, which **passes**; (c) **fails**: 0.18 > 0.10.## Manufacturing 301 · Lesson 2: The surface is a fatigue input

Materials lesson 17 gave fatigue strengths from test bars. This lesson adds a catch: **those bars were polished**. A real part's **surface finish** cuts its fatigue strength, sometimes by half.

### 1. Why roughness matters for fatigue

Fatigue cracks **start at the surface**, at tiny notches (Materials lessons 16–17). A rough surface (tool marks, forge scale, pits) is a **field of ready-made micro-notches**, like the scratch that snaps a glass. It skips part of the crack's "birth" stage.

### 2. The surface factor

**real fatigue strength = polished-bar value × surface factor**

| Surface | Factor (teaching values) | 300 MPa steel becomes |
|---|---|---|
| **Polished** | 1.0 | **300 MPa** |
| **Machined** | ~0.8 | **240 MPa** |
| **Forged** (as-forged skin) | ~0.5 | **150 MPa** |

**The alloy didn't change. Only the surface did.**

### 3. What the animation shows

| Step | What's shown |
|---|---|
| **1. Polished** | 300 MPa fatigue plateau (fully reversed loading), measured on a polished bar |
| **2. Machined** | × 0.8 → 240 MPa |
| **3. Forged** | × 0.5 → 150 MPa |
| **4. Multiply** | apply the factor before claiming a fatigue life |

### 4. Only for fatigue

**Don't apply this to a one-time static yield check.** A single pull to yield doesn't care much about surface roughness. It's **repeated loading** that grows cracks from those notches.

### 5. Stronger steels are punished more

High-strength steels are **more notch-sensitive**, so a rough surface costs them a bigger fraction. These three factors are a teaching set for this 300 MPa steel. A harder steel would get lower factors.

### 6. Practical notes (general practice)

- **Polish or fine-finish the critical spots:** fillets, shoulders, where the bending stress peaks.
- **Shot peening** leaves the surface in **compression**, which slows crack starts. It's often used on springs, gears and axles.
- **As-welded toes and as-forged skins** are classic fatigue starting points.
- Real design codes also apply other factors (part size, load type, temperature, reliability). Surface is just the first one.

### 7. The method

1. **Start from the polished-bar fatigue strength.**
2. **Multiply by the surface factor** for the actual finish (and the steel's strength).
3. **Compare with the stress amplitude** the part sees.
4. **Leave it out of static yield checks.**

**Try one:** using this 300 MPa steel and the page's factors, an axle sees a **220 MPa** fully reversed stress amplitude.

- (a) Machined axle: what's the fatigue strength and the margin (MS)?
- (b) Left with an **as-forged** surface?
- (c) Does the surface finish change its **static** yield check?

You should get (a) 240 MPa, MS = 240/220 − 1 ≈ **0.09** (passes, but thin); (b) 150 MPa, MS ≈ **−0.32**, so it **fails** in fatigue; (c) **no**, surface factors are for fatigue only.## Manufacturing 301 · Lesson 3: How long the arc stays

This ties together the two earlier welding lessons. Both said "more heat per length" makes things worse (a wider HAZ, more bow). This lesson shows **where that heat comes from**, and that **travel speed** is often the knob that's moved.

### 1. The lighter analogy

Hold a lighter under one spot and it chars. Sweep it along and each spot barely warms. **The flame didn't get smaller. It just didn't stay.**

### 2. Heat input per length

**HI = (V × I) ÷ travel speed** (× arc efficiency η)

- **V × I** = electrical power (watts = J/s)
- **Travel speed** in mm/s → result in **J/mm**

Shop shortcut with speed in mm/min: **HI (kJ/mm) = V × I × 60 ÷ (speed × 1,000)**. The 60 is seconds per minute and the 1,000 is J per kJ, not welding constants.

### 3. What the animation shows (20 V, 150 A, one minute of arc)

| Step | Travel | Distance in 1 min | Heat per mm |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Same arc** | | 3,000 W either way | |
| **2. Travel** | 300 mm/min (5 mm/s) | 300 mm | 3,000 ÷ 5 = **0.60 kJ/mm** |
| **3. Heat per mm** | 150 mm/min | 150 mm | **1.20 kJ/mm** |

**Halve the travel speed and each millimetre gets twice the heat.** The current and voltage never changed.

The page uses η = 1, an upper estimate. A real arc delivers less to the workpiece.

### 4. Why it matters (from earlier lessons)

| Doubling the heat input… | Effect |
|---|---|
| HAZ width (Mfg lesson 5) | grows about **√2 ≈ 1.4×** |
| Distortion bow (Mfg 201 lesson 4) | about **2×** |

**A welder who slows down to "get a better bead" can be quietly doubling the heat.**

### 5. The method

1. **Power = V × I.**
2. **Convert travel** to mm/s.
3. **HI = power ÷ speed** (× efficiency).
4. **Check travel speed before turning up the current.**

**Try one:**

- (a) A weld runs at **22 V, 180 A, 250 mm/min**, with arc efficiency **0.8**. What's the heat input?
- (b) At 20 V, 150 A (η = 1), what travel speed gives **0.50 kJ/mm**?
- (c) A welder slows from 300 to 200 mm/min. By what factor does the heat input change?

You should get (a) 3,960 W ÷ 4.17 mm/s ≈ 950 J/mm × 0.8 ≈ **0.76 kJ/mm**; (b) 3,000 ÷ 500 = 6 mm/s = **360 mm/min**; (c) **1.5×** more heat per mm.## Manufacturing 301 · Lesson 4: Yield is a product

This lesson is about a **whole production line**: if every step is "pretty good," how many parts come out **perfect the first time**? Fewer than you'd think.

### 1. The coin analogy

A coin that lands well **98%** of the time:

- 10 clean runs in a row: about **82%**
- 40 clean runs in a row: **under half**

That's **multiplication**, not an average of 98%.

### 2. First-pass yield (FPY)

**FPY = the chance a part survives every step = the product of each step's success rate**

For n identical steps, each with defect chance p:

**FPY = (1 − p)ⁿ**

For different steps, just multiply: 0.99 × 0.95 × 0.98 ≈ **0.92**.

### 3. What the animation shows (each step keeps 98%)

| Step | Steps | FPY |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Each step** | 1 | 98% |
| **2. 10 steps** | 10 | 0.98¹⁰ ≈ **82%** |
| **3. 40 steps** | 40 | 0.98⁴⁰ ≈ **45%** |

**No single step looked bad**, but more than half the parts need rework or scrap after 40 steps.

### 4. Removing a step often beats tightening all of them

Each step you add multiplies in another chance of failure. **Fewer steps** (combining operations, fewer setups, fewer parts in an assembly) can be worth more than squeezing a percent out of every step. **"A design that needs forty perfect things has a yield problem."**

This connects to Engineering lesson 18 (fewer setups) and Manufacturing 201 lesson 5 (every re-fixture is a chance to mislocate).

### 5. Independence is the hopeful case

The formula assumes each step's defects are **unrelated**. In reality, defects often **cluster**: one root cause (a worn fixture, a bad batch of material) feeds several steps. Then the simple product can be wrong in either direction. **Look for shared causes** before trusting the number.

### 6. The method

1. **List the steps** and each one's success rate.
2. **Multiply them**, never average.
3. **Look for steps to remove** before tightening every step.
4. **Check for shared causes** that break the independence assumption.

**Try one:** a part goes through **20 steps at 98%** each.

- (a) What's the first-pass yield?
- (b) Option A: **remove 5 steps** (15 left at 98%). New FPY?
- (c) Option B: keep 20 steps, but **improve every one to 98.5%**. New FPY?
- (d) Which is easier in practice?

You should get (a) 0.98²⁰ ≈ **67%**; (b) 0.98¹⁵ ≈ **74%**; (c) 0.985²⁰ ≈ **74%**, the same; (d) usually **removing 5 steps**, since improving all 20 processes takes far more effort and keeps all 20 sources of trouble.## Manufacturing 301 · Lesson 5: The bond between roads

This lesson is about the **add** act (3D printing): why a printed part is **weaker in one direction**, and how part orientation decides whether that matters. It's the same idea as composites in Materials lesson 23.

### 1. The paper-stack analogy

A stack of papers glued together is strong if you pull a sheet **along its own plane**. Peel one sheet off the next and you're only fighting the **glue**. The paper didn't get weaker. You changed direction.

### 2. A printed part is a stack

FDM printing lays down **roads** (lines of melted plastic), layer on layer. Each new layer has to **fuse** to the one below, and that bond is usually weaker than the plastic itself.

**strength between layers = strength along roads ÷ knockdown**

### 3. What the animation shows

| Step | Direction | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Print** | layers stacked | |
| **2. Along** | pulling along the roads | **40 MPa** (the plastic itself) |
| **3. Peel** | pulling layers apart | 40 ÷ 2 = **20 MPa** (the bond) |

### 4. Knockdown = 1 is a claim, not a measurement

A knockdown of 1 says "the layer bond is as strong as the plastic." **Only say that if you've tested it.** A default of about **2** is a reasonable starting assumption until you measure. Real values depend on material, temperature, speed and cooling.

### 5. The load's direction picks the number

- Load pulls **along** the roads → use **40**.
- Load **peels** layers apart → use **20**.

**Orientation is a design choice.** Printing a bracket on its side instead of upright can move the weak plane **off** the load path (Engineering lesson 7). Write **both the orientation and the knockdown** next to any strength you quote.

### 6. The method

1. **Find which way the load runs** relative to the layers.
2. **Along roads:** full strength. **Across layers:** divide by the knockdown.
3. **Use the measured knockdown**, or about 2 if you haven't measured it. Never 1 by default.
4. **Consider re-orienting** the print so the main stress runs along the roads.

**Try one:** a printed L-bracket's bending puts **15 MPa** of tension on the critical spot. You need a factor of safety of **2**. Road strength is 40 MPa, knockdown 2.

- (a) Printed **upright**, that tension pulls the layers apart. Does it pass?
- (b) Printed **flat**, the tension runs along the roads. Does it pass?

You should get (a) the bond is 20 MPa, so FoS = 20/15 ≈ **1.33** → **fails**; (b) 40/15 ≈ **2.67** → **passes**. Same part, same plastic, just rotated on the print bed.

## Manufacturing 401 · Lesson 1: The slow station

This starts **Manufacturing 401**, which moves from single parts to **whole production lines**. First idea: **a line runs at the speed of its slowest station.**

### 1. The cafeteria analogy

A cafeteria line moves at the pace of the **slowest server**. Hiring help at a station that was already waiting doesn't feed anyone faster.

### 2. The rule

For stations in a row:

**throughput (parts/hour) = 3,600 ÷ slowest station time (seconds)**

The slowest station is the **bottleneck** (or **constraint**). Speeding up any other station **doesn't change the rate**.

### 3. What the animation shows

Three stations: **20 s, 45 s, 30 s**

| Step | Change | Times | Slowest | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. As built** | | 20 / **45** / 30 | 45 s | **80/h** |
| **2. Station 1** | 20 → 10 s | 10 / **45** / 30 | 45 s | **still 80/h** ✘ |
| **3. Station 2** | 45 → 25 s | 20 / 25 / **30** | 30 s | **120/h** ✔ |
| **4. Rate** | the constraint moved to station 3 | | | |

**Speeding station 1 was wasted effort.** It was already sitting idle part of the time.

**Once you fix the bottleneck, another station becomes the bottleneck.** Here the 30 s station is now in charge.

### 4. Ways to relieve a bottleneck (general practice)

- **Speed it up:** better tooling, faster cycle (watch tool life, Mfg 201 lesson 2)
- **Add a parallel machine:** two 45 s machines side by side act like one 22.5 s station
- **Move work** from the bottleneck to an idle station
- **Never let it sit idle:** keep a small buffer of parts in front of it, schedule maintenance elsewhere

An hour lost at the bottleneck is an hour lost for the whole line. An hour lost at an idle station costs almost nothing.

### 5. What the model leaves out

Real lines have **queues, breakdowns and variable cycle times**. This is the clean version of the idea: **work on the constraint.**

### 6. The method

1. **List the station times.**
2. **Find the slowest.** That's the bottleneck.
3. **Rate = 3,600 ÷ bottleneck time.**
4. **Improve only the bottleneck**, then re-check which station is now slowest.

**Try one:** four stations at **35, 50, 40, 25 s**.

- (a) What's the line rate?
- (b) You cut the 25 s station to 15 s. New rate?
- (c) Instead, you add a **second parallel machine** at the 50 s station. New rate, and which station is the bottleneck now?
- (d) What would it take to reach **100 parts/hour**?

You should get (a) 3,600 ÷ 50 = **72/h**; (b) **still 72/h**; (c) that station becomes effectively 25 s, so the 40 s station is the bottleneck and the rate is **90/h**; (d) every station at **36 s or less**, so the 40 s station must also be improved.## Manufacturing 401 · Lesson 2: The tool divided by the count

This revisits the volume economics from Engineering lesson 16, focusing on **how unit cost falls as the batch grows**.

### 1. The cookie-cutter analogy

A cookie cutter is expensive, but each cookie costs only a bit of dough and time. Bake **10** and the cutter is most of each cookie's cost. Bake **10,000** and it almost disappears.

### 2. The formula

**unit cost = recurring cost per part + tooling ÷ number of parts**

| Cost | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| **Recurring** (material + machine minutes) | the same on **every** part, the floor that never moves |
| **Tooling** (die, mould, fixture) | **shared** across the batch |

### 3. What the animation shows

Material **4.00** + cycle time **2.40** = **6.40** per part. Die = **8,000**.

| Step | Batch | Tool per part | Unit cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Floor** | | | 6.40 (never moves) |
| **2. 100 parts** | 100 | 80.00 | **86.40** |
| **3. More parts** | 1,000 | 8.00 | 14.40 |
| **4. 10,000 parts** | 10,000 | 0.80 | **7.20** |

**The press didn't get faster.** The tool was just shared across more parts.

### 4. The lesson: compare at your actual quantity

**An expensive die is a bad idea at 100 parts and an obvious one at 10,000.** Never compare two processes at one quantity and call the answer general.

**Crossover** between a tooled process and an untooled one:
**N = tooling ÷ (untooled cost per part − tooled recurring cost per part)**

### 5. What this simple model leaves out

- **Learning:** in real life the 100th part is usually faster than the 1st.
- **Scrap:** this doesn't divide by yield. Realistically, **cost per good part ≈ unit cost ÷ first-pass yield** (Mfg 301 lesson 4).

### 6. The method

1. **Recurring cost** = material + cycle cost per part.
2. **Add tooling ÷ quantity.**
3. **Compare processes at the quantity you'll really make.**
4. **Find the crossover** if the quantity is uncertain.

**Try one:** the die process above (8,000 tooling + 6.40 per part) vs **CNC machining** at **25.00 per part** with no tooling.

- (a) Which is cheaper at **200** parts?
- (b) At **2,000** parts?
- (c) What's the crossover quantity?
- (d) At 10,000 parts with an **82%** first-pass yield, roughly what's the cost per *good* die-made part?

You should get (a) die = 6.40 + 40 = 46.40 vs CNC 25.00, so **CNC**; (b) die = 6.40 + 4 = **10.40**, so **die**; (c) 8,000 ÷ (25 − 6.40) ≈ **430 parts**; (d) 7.20 ÷ 0.82 ≈ **8.78**.## Manufacturing 401 · Lesson 3: Parts you can delete

This lesson is about **design for assembly (DFA)**: each extra part costs handling time, so **fewer parts usually means faster, cheaper assembly**, as long as the product still works.

### 1. The toy analogy

A toy held together with **twelve screws** takes longer to assemble than one held by **two snaps**, even if the screws are cheap. Most of the time goes into **picking up and placing**, not the price of the part.

### 2. The teaching model

**assembly time = base time + time per extra part × (parts − 1)**

With **20 s base** and **8 s per extra part**.

### 3. What the animation shows

| Step | Parts | Calculation | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. 6 parts** | 6 | 20 + 5 × 8 | **60 s** |
| **2. 3 parts** | 3 | 20 + 2 × 8 | **36 s** |
| **3. Delete** | | | **24 s saved** |

Each deleted part is **one 8 s handling** you no longer do.

### 4. Delete only what the function doesn't need

**The formula counts parts, not function.** It doesn't check whether the joint still holds.

**Example:** replacing three screws with one snap saves 16 s, and **might ruin the joint**. Checking strength (Engineering lessons 13–14) is still your job.

**A common DFA test** (from standard DFA practice): a part needs to stay separate only if:

- it **moves** relative to the parts around it
- it must be a **different material** (insulator, seal, wear surface)
- it must come apart for **assembly, service or replacement**

If none of those apply, it's a candidate to **merge** into a neighbouring part.

### 5. Bonus: fewer parts helps yield too

Every part is another handling step and another chance of a defect. **Fewer parts = higher first-pass yield** (Mfg 301 lesson 4).

### 6. The method

1. **Count the parts.**
2. **For each one, ask:** does it move, does it need a different material, does it need to come off for service?
3. **If not, try merging or deleting it.**
4. **Recompute assembly time.**
5. **Verify** the joint and function still hold.

**Try one:** a bracket assembly has **9 parts**: a bracket, a plate, 4 bolts, 2 washers and a spacer.

- (a) What's the assembly time?
- (b) A redesign merges the spacer into the bracket and uses 2 flanged bolts (no washers), leaving **5 parts**. New time?
- (c) At **10,000 assemblies a year**, how many hours does that save?
- (d) What must you check before approving it?

You should get (a) 20 + 8 × 8 = **84 s**; (b) 20 + 4 × 8 = **52 s**; (c) 32 s × 10,000 ≈ **89 hours**; (d) that **2 bolts** carry the load with margin (bolt shear and bearing, Engineering lessons 8 and 14), and that the flanged bolts spread the clamping load like the washers did.## Manufacturing 401 · Lesson 4: Scrap you already paid for

The last lesson in 301 showed yield falls as steps multiply. This lesson puts a **price** on it: scrapped parts were already paid for, so **the good parts have to carry that cost**.

### 1. The bakery analogy

Bake 100 loaves and throw 20 away. **All 100** used flour and oven time. The 80 you sell carry the cost of all 100, so each costs **more** than one loaf's ingredients.

### 2. The rule

**cost per good part = process cost per part ÷ yield**

**Divide, don't subtract.** Taking 20% off the cost ("10 − 2 = 8") pretends the scrap **saved** you money.

### 3. What the animation shows

Every part costs **10** to process.

| Step | What's shown |
|---|---|
| **1. Process** | 10 parts × 10 = **100 spent** |
| **2. Scrap** | 8 good ship, 2 scrapped |
| **3. Divide** | 100 ÷ 8 = 10 ÷ 0.80 = **12.50 per good part** (not 8) |

**At 95% yield:** 10 ÷ 0.95 ≈ **10.53**.

### 4. What improving yield is worth

**saving per good part = cost ÷ old yield − cost ÷ new yield**

80% → 95%: 12.50 − 10.53 ≈ **1.97 per part**. Multiply by annual volume and you have the budget that justifies a better fixture, tool or inspection.

### 5. Rework is a third path

This calculation assumes bad parts are **thrown away**, losing the full process cost. If a bad part can be **reworked**, it costs somewhere between nothing and a full remake. **Say so** if rework exists, and calculate it separately.

### 6. The method

1. **Process cost per part** (every part started).
2. **Divide by yield** → cost per good part.
3. **Value a yield improvement** as the difference, times volume.
4. **If rework exists**, handle it explicitly. Don't use the pure division.

**Try one:**

- (a) A part costs **15** to process with **90%** yield. What's the cost per good part?
- (b) A new fixture raises yield to **98%**. What's the saving per good part, and per year at **20,000** good parts?
- (c) Back at 90% yield: suppose **7 of the 10** bad parts per 100 can be **reworked for 4 each** (the other 3 are scrapped). What's the cost per good part now?

You should get (a) 15 ÷ 0.90 ≈ **16.67**; (b) 15 ÷ 0.98 ≈ 15.31, saving ≈ **1.36 per part**, about **27,000 per year**; (c) per 100 started: 1,500 + 7 × 4 = 1,528 spent for 97 good parts ≈ **15.75 each**.## Manufacturing 401 · Lesson 5: The pace already set

Lesson 1 found the line's speed (the bottleneck). This lesson asks the other half: **how fast does the line *need* to go?** That pace comes from **customer demand**, not from the machines.

### 1. The bakery analogy

You owe **50 loaves** in a **400-minute** morning, so that's one loaf every **8 minutes**, like it or not. Owe **200** and it's one every **2 minutes**. An oven that takes 4 minutes per loaf is **early** for the first order and **late** for the second.

### 2. Takt time

**takt = available minutes ÷ parts owed**

- **Takt** = the pace demand sets
- **Cycle time** = how long the station actually takes

| Comparison | Meaning |
|---|---|
| cycle **<** takt | **slack** each part |
| cycle **=** takt | exactly on pace |
| cycle **>** takt | **late** by (cycle − takt) on **every** part, and it **stacks up** |

### 3. What the animation shows

400 minutes available, a **4-minute** station.

| Step | Demand | Takt | vs 4-min station |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. 50 parts** | 50 | 400 ÷ 50 = **8 min** | **4 min slack** each ✔ |
| **2. 200 parts** | 200 | 400 ÷ 200 = **2 min** | **2 min late** on every part ✘ |
| **3. Compare** | 100 parts | 4 min | exactly on pace |

### 4. Don't speed up a station that's already inside takt

At 50 parts, making the 4-minute station faster **doesn't serve the customer**. They only need one every 8 minutes. If a station is **outside** takt, nothing else on the line will hide it.

### 5. Putting 401 together

- **Lesson 1:** the line runs at its **bottleneck**.
- **Lesson 5:** the customer needs **takt**.
- **The line meets demand only if the bottleneck time ≤ takt.**
- **Lesson 4:** if yield is less than 100%, you must **start more parts** than you owe, which tightens takt.

### 6. The method

1. **Available time:** shift time minus breaks and planned stops.
2. **Takt = available ÷ demand** (divide demand by yield if there's scrap).
3. **Compare every station's cycle with takt**, especially the bottleneck.
4. **Report slack or late per part.**
5. **Fix stations over takt** (parallel machines, move work). Leave the ones under it alone.

**Try one:** an 8-hour shift with **30 minutes of breaks**. Demand: **90 parts**. Three stations at **4, 6 and 3 minutes**.

- (a) What's the takt?
- (b) Which station is late, by how much per part, and how many parts short is the shift?
- (c) With a **95%** first-pass yield, what does takt become?
- (d) What's one fix?

You should get (a) 450 ÷ 90 = **5 min**; (b) the **6-minute** station is 1 min late per part, and it can only make 450 ÷ 6 = 75, so **15 short**; (c) you must start about 95 parts, so takt ≈ 450 ÷ 95 ≈ **4.7 min**; (d) add a **parallel machine** at the 6-minute station (effectively 3 min), or move some of its work to the 3-minute station.
